Why the River

Which River, Which Ship, Which Month

Choose the river for the scenery and the month for the water. The Rhine runs through UNESCO's 65 km Upper Middle Rhine gorge of castles; the Danube links Vienna and Budapest; the Rhône carries Lyon-to-Avignon wine country; the Douro climbs terraced vineyards through five locks; the Seine pairs Paris with Normandy. Sailings typically run 7 nights, and April and October are the shoulder months.

The case for a river is simple: you unpack once and wake most mornings in a new town, often in a new country, with the ship moored a walk or a short ride from the old centre. The trade-off is time ashore. A typical day gives a morning or an afternoon in port, not both, so a river cruise is a comfortable overview of a region rather than a deep stay in any one city. Most travelers fix that with land nights before or after, covered below.

The ships differ less in size than ocean ships do, because the waterway sets the limits. Locks and low bridges cap their length, beam and height, which is why a Douro ship is shorter than a Rhine ship and why sun decks close under some bridges. What separates them is the cabin, the kitchen, the excursions included and how many guests share each. The Rivers

Five Rivers, Five Different Weeks

Each river has its own scenery, its own season and its own size of ship. Facts below are from the UNESCO World Heritage listings and the waterway authorities, checked October 2026.

The Rhine: Castles and the Gorge
The classic first river: Amsterdam or Basel at either end, with Cologne, Koblenz and Strasbourg between. Its centrepiece is the Upper Middle Rhine Valley, a UNESCO-listed 65 km stretch between Bingen, Rüdesheim and Koblenz, crowded with castles and vineyards and usually sailed by day. Viking's Rhine Getaway is a typical shape: 8 days, Basel to Amsterdam. The Rhine is also the river most exposed to late-summer low water.

The Rhône & Saône: Lyon to Avignon
The wine river. Ships run south from Lyon, where the Saône meets the Rhône, past the hill of Hermitage at Tain-l'Hermitage to Avignon, with Châteauneuf-du-Pape just north of it; many itineraries also run up the Saône toward Burgundy. The appellations are mapped by Inter Rhône, and private estate tastings booked apart from the ship's excursions are the usual upgrade. The Compagnie Nationale du Rhône runs 14 large locks between Lyon and the sea. For land days, see Burgundy and Lyon wine trips and Provence and Avignon on land.

The Douro: Terraces and High Locks
The steepest scenery of the five. Ships leave Porto for the Alto Douro Wine Region, terraced port vineyards listed by UNESCO in 2001, and sail to the Spanish border at Barca d'Alva and Vega de Terón, with Salamanca as a day trip. Five dams and locks lift the river, the highest at Carrapatelo at about 35 metres, and the locks hold ships to about 83 metres, per the waterway authority APDL, so Douro ships are smaller. For the land side, see Porto and the Douro Valley by land.

The Ship

Choosing the Ship

Six questions that separate two ships on the same river and the same dates. The answers are in each line's deck plans and terms, not its brochure photographs.

⬡
French Balcony or Full Balcony
A French balcony is a floor-to-ceiling window that opens to a railing; a full balcony has floor space for two chairs. Some ships offer both, sometimes in one cabin. The deck plan shows which cabins have which, and which sit on the lowest deck, where windows are fixed and near the waterline.
⬡
The Sun Deck and Low Bridges
Ships are built low to pass under bridges, and on some stretches the crew lowers the wheelhouse, folds the railings and closes the sun deck. That is normal. It matters on the Rhine gorge day, when the open deck is the point of the sailing.
⬡
Where the Ship Moors
Berths are shared, and ships often tie up alongside one another, so the view from a cabin can be the next ship's cabin and the way ashore can run through its lobby. Moorings also vary within a city: Vienna has two, set out below.
⬡
Included Tours or Private Guides
Most lines include a guided walk in each port, run in groups with headsets. A private guide or driver costs extra but sets the pace, and is the usual route to wine estates or sites outside the group programme. Check the return-to-ship time first.
⬡
Low or High Water Plans
When the river runs too low or too high, lines swap guests between sister ships either side of the problem stretch, add coach transfers, or turn the ship into a moored hotel. Read the line's terms on itinerary changes and refunds before booking a July to October Rhine sailing.
⬡
Land Nights Either Side
Embarkation cities are worth a night or two of their own: Amsterdam or Basel on the Rhine, Vienna or Budapest on the Danube, Lyon on the Rhône, Porto on the Douro, Paris on the Seine. A night first also absorbs a late transatlantic arrival.
When to Go

River Season by Month

The month sets the crowds, the light and the water level. Shoulder months win on all three.

April
Shoulder · Best Window
Spring water levels, blossom in the Wachau and fewer ships at each berth. Giverny opens on 1 April for Seine sailings.
May – August
Peak
Long days and full ships. By late summer the Rhine can run low: the Kaub gauge on ELWIS, the German waterways administration's service, is the reading lines watch, and in August 2026 it fell below its 2018 record low.
September – October
Shoulder · Best Window
Harvest on every wine river. In the Douro the vindima runs mainly through September into early October, per Visit Portugal. Low water can linger into autumn after a dry summer.
Late November – December
Christmas Markets
Rhine and Danube sailings timed to the markets. Nuremberg's Christkindlesmarkt opens the Friday before the first Sunday of Advent: 27 November to 24 December in 2026. Short daylight and cold decks.

Waterway figures, opening dates and itinerary lengths checked October 2026. River levels change week to week; check the line's current notices before you sail.

The Rhine or the Danube, because they have the most departures, the most ships and the best-known sights. The Rhine gives castles and the 65 km UNESCO gorge; the Danube gives Vienna and Budapest. Choose the Rhône for wine, the Douro for landscape once the classics are done, and the Seine for a round trip from Paris.

The Rhine is about scenery and small towns; the Danube is about capital cities. A Rhine week builds to the castle-lined gorge between Bingen and Koblenz, with Cologne and Strasbourg as the big stops. A Danube week builds to Vienna and Budapest, with the Wachau valley's vineyards between Melk and Krems as its scenic day.

April and October, the shoulder months, for fewer ships, milder days and lower water-level risk. September and October add the grape harvest on the Rhône, Rhine and Douro. Summer is warmest and busiest, and late summer carries the highest low-water risk on the Rhine. Late November and December are for the Christmas markets.

The line changes the itinerary: a ship swap, a coach around the problem stretch, or nights moored in one port. Low water stops ships on shallow stretches such as Kaub on the Middle Rhine, whose gauge is published on ELWIS; high water stops them passing under bridges. Each line's terms say what it refunds, so read them before booking.

Budapest's moorings line the city embankments, and Lyon's sit on the Rhône quays within walking distance of the centre.
