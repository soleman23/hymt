Every year I have a version of the same conversation. A client is planning their first real Europe trip — or their first trip in many years, or their honeymoon, or their anniversary — and they arrive at the initial call with a list. London. Paris. Rome. Florence. Maybe Venice. Maybe Barcelona if we can fit it. Two weeks. They have worked out the rough routing. They want to know if it's feasible.

It is technically feasible. It is also one of the least satisfying ways to spend two weeks in Europe, and the people who have done it know this retrospectively in a way that is almost universal. What they describe afterward is logistics. What they remember is trains and airports and suitcases and two nights in each place — just enough time to see the famous things from the outside and eat one good meal before moving on.

I do not tell clients they can't do this. I explain what it produces, explain what the alternative produces, and let them make the decision with accurate information. Most of them change the plan. This piece is the version of that explanation I give before the conversation even starts.

"Covering Europe in two weeks doesn't make you well-traveled in Europe. It makes you familiar with the lobbies of hotels you were too tired to enjoy, the queues of attractions you were too rushed to absorb, and the airports you spent three days of your vacation moving through."

What the multi-city itinerary actually produces

The logic of the grand tour is coverage. If you've been to Paris, Rome, Florence, and Barcelona, you've "done Europe" in some meaningful sense — you've been there, you've seen the major things, you have a framework for the continent. This logic is not entirely wrong. A first exposure to several different European urban environments does create a map that subsequent trips can fill in with depth.

The problem is what it costs. In a fourteen-day trip covering six cities, roughly two to three days are consumed by transit — flights, trains, transfers, packing and unpacking, orienting to each new location. Of the remaining eleven days, two nights in each city means one full day and one partial day per destination before departure morning. One day in Paris is enough to see the Eiffel Tower from the outside, attempt the Louvre for three hours, eat at one restaurant, and feel you have been to Paris. It is not enough to be in Paris.

The texture of a city — the neighborhoods that aren't on the tourist map, the specific food culture, the pace of how people actually use the space, the incidental encounters that produce the memories you still have twenty years later — requires time that two nights doesn't buy. You don't notice the light on the Seine at different hours when you're there for thirty-six. You don't find the restaurant in the 11th that the people who live there actually eat at when your map is entirely composed of TripAdvisor's top-twenty.

The hotel math doesn't work either

At the price point where travel is genuinely excellent — hotels that are worth staying in, restaurants that are worth the reservation, experiences that require advance booking — the multi-city itinerary is also wasteful in a specific way.

The first night in any new hotel is a calibration night. You learn the room, the breakfast timing, which elevator to use, what the light is like, how the shower works. Some travelers love this ritual; most simply absorb the friction without noticing it as friction. But in a two-night stay, that calibration night is half your stay. A property that would reveal itself over four nights — the morning when you have the terrace to yourself, the evening concierge who turns out to be a resource, the small detail of the room that becomes your favorite thing about the trip — never has the time to develop.

The same principle applies to the relationship between the hotel and the city. An exceptional hotel in Rome knows its neighborhood in a way that produces introductions, reservations, and access that a standard booking in the same hotel doesn't. That relationship takes a couple of days to establish. On a two-night stay, you leave before it becomes useful.

What one region for two weeks actually produces

Tuscany for fourteen days. Or the Amalfi Coast and Rome. Or Paris and Provence. Or the Swiss Alps and Lake Como. The specific region matters less than the principle: depth rather than coverage.

In fourteen days in Tuscany, you can stay in two or three places — say, Florence for four nights, a hill-town agriturismo for four nights, Siena for three nights — and have the experience the continent is actually capable of producing. You find the restaurant in Florence you'd return to. You drive through the Val d'Orcia on a Tuesday morning when the light is perfect and you have nowhere to be until dinner. You sit at a table outside a wine bar in Siena's Piazza del Campo for two hours and watch the pigeons and the tourists and the Sienese going about their day, and you understand something about how a city actually functions that a forty-minute stop never reveals.

The counter-argument is always: but I haven't seen Paris, Rome, Barcelona. This is true. You also haven't seen Kyoto, Oaxaca, Istanbul, Cape Town, or a thousand other places worth seeing. The purpose of a trip is not to reduce the list of places you haven't been to. It's to have a specific, irreplaceable experience in the time you have. Maximum coverage and maximum experience are not the same objective, and optimizing for one consistently degrades the other.

The exception: when the grand tour is correct

There are cases where the multi-city sweep is the right call. If you are genuinely uncertain about where you want to spend more time — if this is a reconnaissance trip, an intentional sampler before deciding where to go deeper on a future visit — then covering ground deliberately makes sense. The 21-year-old on a first Europe trip who sleeps in hostels, takes night trains, and moves fast through six cities is gathering raw material. The reconnaissance model serves a genuine purpose.

The problem arises when the reconnaissance model is applied to a trip that is supposed to be the trip itself. When someone is taking their honeymoon, or their one big vacation of the year, or a significant anniversary trip, and they build it as a coverage exercise — that's a mismatch between the trip's meaning and its structure. The trip that produces lifelong memories almost never does so by being comprehensive.

How to tell a client they've built the wrong trip

I don't, directly. What I do is ask a single question: what do you want to feel at the end of this trip? Rested and replenished, or stimulated and informed? Deeply present in a few places, or broadly oriented across many? Neither answer is wrong. But most people, when they actually consider the question rather than defaulting to the itinerary they've been imagining since they started planning, discover that what they want from the trip and what they've built are different things.

Europe is not going anywhere. The cities will be there for a second and third trip. The Uffizi will still have the Botticellis. The Amalfi road will still be narrow and dramatic and impossible to drive quickly. Paris will still be Paris. The question isn't whether you'll get to see these things eventually. It's whether this trip — this specific allocation of time and money and attention — is the right instrument for seeing them, or whether slowing down and going deeper in one place would produce something more worth having.

Most people, given time to actually think about it, already know the answer.

How we approach this in planning

When a client arrives with a multi-city list, the first question we ask isn't "can we make this work?" It's "what are you actually trying to get from this trip?" That single conversation changes the itinerary more often than not — and the trips that come out of it are consistently the ones clients come back to us to plan again.

The Alternative, Concretely

Two weeks in one country instead of six cities is the entire argument. In practice that usually means Italy — Rome, Tuscany and one stretch of coast — or France, Paris paired with a single region rather than four.
