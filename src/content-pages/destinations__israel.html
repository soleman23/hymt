The Country

Israel Runs on the Calendar,
Not on the Map.

The two windows that work are March to May and October to November. A first trip runs 8 to 10 days: Jerusalem wants 4 nights, Tel Aviv two or three, and the Galilee or the Dead Sea takes the rest. Public transport stops from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall, Israel Railways included, so the week is built around Shabbat rather than the other way round.

Two calendars run here at once. The weekly one stops the trains, the buses and most of the shops for twenty-five hours from Friday sunset. The annual one is three religious calendars at the same time, Hebrew, Christian and Muslim, each moving against the Gregorian year and against each other, so last year's dates are wrong for this year. An itinerary written without both open in front of you will lose a day, and it will usually be the day earmarked for the Old City.

Jerusalem is the reason most people come and the place most trips underbook. The walled Old City covers under a square kilometre and holds four quarters, and the Old City of Jerusalem and its Walls has been on the World Heritage List since 1981. Within fifteen minutes of each other sit the Western Wall, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and the Via Dolorosa. Which of those you understand, and how much of the layering under them you get, depends almost entirely on who is walking with you.

Everything else is a counterweight to that. Tel Aviv is an hour away and behaves like a different country: UNESCO counts some 4,000 buildings in its 1930s International Style core, Shabbat barely registers, and the beach is a short walk from it. North is the Galilee, green and agricultural. South is Masada, the Dead Sea and a desert most visitors never reach. Pick two of the three, or take twelve days.

Two things decide how this trip goes: who guides you in the Old City, and whether the week was written against the calendar or in spite of it.

The Old City Guide Decision
Jerusalem is the one place on this site where the guide matters more than the hotel. A licensed guide will get you to the Western Wall, the Holy Sepulchre and the Via Dolorosa in a morning and give you the dates. A specialist will explain the 1852 Status Quo that still governs which Christian community maintains which stair inside the Sepulchre, why the Armenian Quarter keeps its own hours, and what changed above and below ground across three thousand years on the same rock. Same walking route, two different cities. We match the guide to what you came to understand, and book them long before the hotel.
⬡
The Calendar, Built In First
We write the week against your specific dates before a single hotel is held. Shabbat removes the trains and most intercity transport from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall. Yom Kippur stops the country outright and closes Ben Gurion Airport for roughly twenty-five hours. Passover fills the hotels for a week. Ramadan changes the Muslim Quarter's rhythm, and Western and Orthodox Easter fall on different dates, which decides which procession you see. Each is fine to travel through when it is planned for, and expensive to meet by accident on a Friday afternoon with a driver who has gone home.
⬡
Where the Nights Actually Go
The country is small, so almost everything is technically a day trip from Jerusalem or Tel Aviv. That is the trap. Masada at sunrise from Jerusalem means leaving in the middle of the night; from a Dead Sea hotel it is a normal early start. The Galilee from the coast is a long drive for a shallow look at a region that repays three nights. We spend the planning argument on where you sleep rather than on what you see, because on a map this size the sleeping decides the seeing.
⬡
Bookings That Can Move
This region asks more of a booking structure than most, and the answer is not to plan less but to plan reversibly. When to Go

Israel by Season

Spring and Autumn
March – May · October – November
The two windows where the whole country works at once. Jerusalem is walkable at midday, the Galilee is green rather than burnt, and the Dead Sea and the Negev allow a full day outside instead of a dawn raid. April and October are the strongest single months. The catch is that both windows collide with the busiest holidays: Passover and Easter in spring, Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur and Sukkot in autumn. Hotels in Jerusalem go months ahead around all of them, and the good Old City guides go earlier than that.
Best Overall Window
Summer
June – September
Two countries again. The Mediterranean coast is hot but civilised, and Tel Aviv, Akko and Caesarea are perfectly good with a beach afternoon built in. The Dead Sea, Masada and the Negev are not: WMO normals put the rift valley past 40°C, and everything down there compresses into the two hours after sunrise. Jerusalem is bearable, dry rather than humid, and cooler after dark than the coast. If the trip has to fall here, go coast-and-Jerusalem and leave the desert for another year.
Winter
December – February
Cold and wet in Jerusalem, which surprises people who packed for the Middle East. The hills take real rain and occasionally snow, the Galilee is green and empty, and the light is good. This is the one season when the Dead Sea, the Negev and Eilat are the warm option rather than the punishing one, which makes a north-cool, south-warm itinerary work better than at any other time. Christmas and Orthodox Christmas fill Jerusalem on different dates; the rest of the season is the quietest and cheapest of the year.

Public transport, most shops, and much of Jerusalem, from sunset on Friday until nightfall on Saturday. Israel Railways does not run and intercity buses stop, so a Saturday intercity train is not a plan. Tel Aviv is the exception: much of the city carries on, restaurants open, and shared taxis keep moving. In practice Shabbat is not a lost day, it is a differently shaped one: the Christian and Armenian sites in the Old City are open, hotels serve, and a car and driver booked in advance covers the transport gap. What it rules out is a late-Friday transfer, a Saturday intercity train and a Saturday supermarket run. Service times shift with sunset through the year; confirm before booking. Yom Kippur and Passover change everything; Rosh Hashanah, Sukkot, Ramadan and Easter change enough to matter. Ramadan reshapes the Muslim Quarter's day and its evenings. Western and Orthodox Easter fall on different dates, which decides which procession you can be present for. Four nights, and the Old City is only about half of them. One full day covers the walls and the four quarters with a guide who can hold the layers without flattening them. A second takes the Mount of Olives, the City of David and the tunnels beneath the Western Wall plaza, which need booking ahead. Two nights buys the monuments and none of the understanding, a poor trade in the one city where understanding is the point.

Most visa-exempt travellers now need an ETA-IL, approved online before departure, on top of a valid passport.
