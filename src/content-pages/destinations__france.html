Paris repays the planner in a way that few other cities in the world do. A guide who has spent twenty years walking the Marais knows which gallery hung a new show this week and which pastry chef the working chefs go to before their own service starts. The bistrot at the corner of rue Saint-Sabin and rue Sedaine has been serving the same steak-frites menu since 1957, takes no reservations, and is unknown to every English-language guidebook because the owner has no interest in being reviewed. The difference between a Paris trip booked through standard channels and one built around two or three people who actually know the city is the difference between visiting Paris and being in Paris.

Provence rewards slow travel by rental car through the markets, the perched villages of the Luberon, and the lavender fields whose flowering window varies year to year by two or three weeks based on rainfall and temperature. Knowing exactly when the Sault and Valensole plateau lavender is in flower in your travel window — and which year produces a longer or shorter season — is the difference between a Provence trip timed for the photographs and one that arrives a week early or a week late. We track this annually with growers in the region.

Bordeaux is widely misunderstood. The wine country is divided by the Gironde estuary into the right bank (St-Émilion, Pomerol, limestone plateau, Merlot-dominant) and the left bank (Médoc, gravel terraces, Cabernet Sauvignon-dominant) — and the difference between the two is significant enough that the average traveler should be visiting both and understanding which is which. The châteaux range from those open to walk-in visitors with structured tours to those that admit guests only through a négociant introduction. The latter category produces the wine country experience that travelers fly to Bordeaux to have.

What We Add to France Planning

France is the country where the food specialist, the wine introduction, and the property selection have the largest combined effect on the trip. Knowing which person to ask makes the planning work.

⬡
The Guide Who Knows the Chef
Paris and Lyon both have networks of food guides who have eaten in their cities professionally for fifteen or twenty years, who know which kitchen has a new chef this season, which bistrot is taking reservations through a side door, and which restaurant the working chefs in the neighborhood actually go to on their day off. These guides do not advertise. They are accessed through introduction. We hold those introductions.
⬡
Driving Provence Properly
Provence requires a rental car, but more specifically it requires knowing which routes through the Luberon and the Vaucluse to drive in what order, which villages still have an active weekly market (a meaningful proportion no longer do), and which hours of the day produce the photography light that justifies the trip. The lavender field timing varies enough year-to-year that a fixed-date itinerary published months in advance can be five days off the actual peak. We track this annually with regional growers.
⬡
Wine Access
The Bordeaux châteaux divide into three categories: those that accept walk-in visitors with structured guided tours, those that require a négociant introduction, and those that admit guests only through long-standing private relationships. The wine country experience that justifies a trip to Bordeaux is overwhelmingly in the second and third categories. The same structure exists in Burgundy, Champagne, and the Rhône. We maintain the introductions that open the second-category visits and we coordinate the third where appropriate.
⬡
Paris vs the Regions
Paris and the rest of France are essentially different countries. The Parisian week operates on its own restaurant rhythms, museum schedules, and neighborhood seasonality (in August the city partially empties of Parisians and the energy changes entirely). The regions operate on agricultural calendars — harvest, festival, market days. Treating France as one destination produces an itinerary that fights both systems. We structure trips around the right rhythm for each leg.
When to Go

France by Season

Spring Season
May – June
The best general France window. Paris is warm enough for terrace dining without yet being summer-hot. The Provence countryside is in full wildflower bloom, with poppies in May and the early lavender beginning at lower altitudes in late June. Loire Valley gardens are at their peak. Markets across the country show the spring vegetable rotation — asparagus, artichokes, fresh peas, early strawberries. Tourist pressure is below July and August peaks, accommodation rates are below summer levels, and the weather is reliably warm without the August humidity that affects Paris specifically. For a first France trip or for travelers who want broad regional coverage, May and early June is the most consistently rewarding window.
Best Overall Window
Harvest Season
September – October
France's harvest season is one of the great travel calendars in the world. Grape harvest runs September across Bordeaux, Burgundy, and Champagne, with the precise timing varying by appellation and vintage conditions. The light in Provence and the Loire turns autumnal in late September, and the photographic conditions improve as the summer haze clears. Restaurant culture transitions from the summer terrace menu to the autumn rotation (mushrooms, game, the year's first oysters). Tourist pressure drops 30–40% from August. For wine-focused or food-focused trips, September is unambiguously the correct month.
Peak & Off Season
July–August & November–April
July and August are France's domestic vacation period as much as the international peak — Paris partially empties of Parisians, while the coastal and Provençal regions fill with French families and become genuinely crowded. The Côte d'Azur in August requires booking 6+ months ahead for top properties. November through April is the off season — Paris remains a full-year city, the Champagne and Burgundy harvest seasons are over, the Alsace Christmas markets in December are extraordinary, and rates across the country drop significantly. Ski season in the French Alps runs December through March at the major resorts.

Common Questions

France — FAQ

Questions we hear most when planning France — from Paris timing to Provence driving, Bordeaux wine access, and how to combine Paris with the regions.

How many days should I allocate to Paris?+
A minimum of four nights for a first visit — enough to cover the major museum circuit (Louvre, Musée d'Orsay, Picasso, one of the smaller specialist museums), three or four neighborhood walks with a food or culture guide, and the breathing room to repeat a meal you liked rather than feeling obligated to try a new restaurant every night. Five to seven nights is the more comfortable range for a real Paris immersion. Less than three nights produces a checklist rather than an experience.
Is a car necessary in Provence?+
Yes — Provence by guided coach or by exclusively staying in Aix or Avignon misses approximately 70% of what makes Provence Provence. The Luberon villages, the Valensole plateau, the markets in the smaller towns (Lourmarin, L'Isle-sur-la-Sorgue), and the wine country between Châteauneuf-du-Pape and the Côtes du Rhône all require driving. The roads are excellent and the driving is one of the most pleasant in Europe — small roads, low traffic outside of the highway. We strongly recommend the self-drive option for any client comfortable driving abroad.
When is the best time to visit Bordeaux?+
September during the harvest is the definitive window — the vineyards are active, the producers are present at the châteaux (rather than on summer holiday), and the autumn light across the Médoc is the photography most travelers expect when they imagine wine country. May through June is also excellent, with vines fully leafed out and the spring tastings of the previous vintage just released. July and August are warm and many smaller producers reduce their visiting hours; the major classified growths remain open but feel more commercial than at harvest. November through March is the dormant season — beautiful in a stripped-down sense but with significantly reduced producer access.
Is the Loire Valley by bicycle a fitness commitment?+
The Loire à Vélo route is flat, well-marked, and designed for casual cyclists rather than serious riders. Distances between the major châteaux are 15–30 km — within reach for anyone comfortable on a bicycle for a morning. E-bikes are widely available and significantly reduce the fitness component for hilly side-routes. We schedule the rides to allow rest days between the cycling days and book accommodation in villages on the route so luggage transfers handle the gear. Total mileage across a 5-day cycling segment is typically 100–150 km — substantial but not demanding for an average traveler.
Is Alsace underrated for first-time France travelers?+
Yes, significantly. Alsace combines Renaissance villages, world-class Gewürztraminer and Riesling production, distinctive regional cuisine (choucroute, tarte flambée, baeckeoffe), and the most architecturally distinctive city in eastern France (Strasbourg). The wine route between Colmar and Strasbourg is one of the most rewarding driving routes in Europe. For travelers who have done Paris and want a regional France trip with strong food, wine, and architectural content in close geographic concentration, Alsace is consistently the recommendation that surprises most.
How significant is the language barrier in France?+
Less than the stereotype suggests, with one practical caveat. At any property we book — boutique hotel, chambre d'hôte, restaurant of meaningful standard — staff speak excellent English. The notion that the French are unwelcoming to non-French speakers is largely outdated; opening with "bonjour" and attempting basic French phrases produces consistently warm responses. The caveat: in smaller villages in Provence, the Dordogne, and the countryside generally, English fluency drops significantly. For self-drive itineraries in the regions, a translation app and basic French politeness phrases cover the gap entirely.
What are the realistic budget expectations for food and wine in France?+
A two-Michelin or three-Michelin dinner in Paris will run €300–€600 per person with wine pairings. A serious bistrot dinner — the format we typically recommend over the multi-star experience — runs €60–€100 per person with a bottle of wine to share. Lunch menus across France ("formule midi") are dramatically cheaper than dinner at the same restaurants and represent the best value-to-quality ratio in French dining. Wine in restaurants is marked up less aggressively than in the US (typically 2.0–2.5x, vs 3–4x in American restaurants), making it economical to drink well. In the wine regions, cellar door prices for the same wine are often 40–60% below retail.
When is the worst time to visit Paris?+
August is the month most Parisians take their summer vacation, which produces a paradoxical effect: many of the city's best restaurants, bakeries, and small shops close for two to four weeks. The Métro is less crowded, the streets are emptier, and the tourist experience is in many ways smoother — but the actual depth of Paris is reduced. For travelers who want the bistrot culture and the deep food experience, August is genuinely a worse month than September. The exception: travelers who specifically want the calmer summer Paris with longer daylight (sunset is past 9:30pm) and lower domestic competition for tables at the restaurants that do stay open.
