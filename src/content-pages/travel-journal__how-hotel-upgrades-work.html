Every few months, someone tells me about the email they sent a hotel before their anniversary trip — letting the property know what they were celebrating, how much the trip meant, how long they'd been planning it. Sometimes they got a bottle of wine. Usually they got nothing beyond what they booked. They're always slightly surprised, and I'm never surprised at all.

Hotel upgrades are not rewards for sentimentality. They are inventory decisions made by a revenue manager, often 24 to 48 hours before arrival, based on a set of variables that have almost nothing to do with the reason you're traveling and almost everything to do with how you're booked and who you are in the property's system. Understanding those variables is the difference between travelers who receive upgrades consistently and travelers who receive them occasionally, by accident.

"An upgrade is an inventory decision. The hotel is asking: who can we move to a better room at no cost to us, in a way that creates goodwill and fills a gap? Your job is to be the obvious answer to that question."

The actual mechanics

At most hotels — particularly in the luxury and upper-upscale segment — complimentary upgrades are distributed during the daily pre-arrival meeting, typically 24 to 48 hours out. The front office manager reviews arrivals against the inventory: which room categories are oversold, which superior categories have availability, which arrivals are flagged by the CRM as VIP, returning, or advisor-referred.

The hotel is not being generous. It is solving a logistics problem. If the property is sold out of standard rooms but has two junior suites sitting empty, it is better for everyone — including the property — to upgrade a valued arriving guest into one of those suites than to leave it dark. The upgrade costs the hotel nothing in incremental revenue, generates goodwill, and fills a room that wasn't going to be sold anyway. The question they are asking is not "who deserves this?" — it is "who is the most obvious person to move?"

What puts you in that position

Several things determine whether you appear on that short list, and only some of them are within your control.

Booking channel is the most important variable most travelers don't control. When you book directly through a hotel's website, you are one transaction in a system processing thousands per day. When a recognized travel advisor books on your behalf — particularly through Virtuoso, Signature Travel Network, or an equivalent consortium — the reservation arrives flagged as an advisor booking, with a note attached. The property knows that advisor's client base. They know that treating the booking well reflects on a relationship they want to maintain. That is a categorically different arrival status than a direct booking, and it is the single most reliable driver of complimentary upgrades at luxury properties.

Loyalty status matters at the properties where you have it — and matters less than most programs suggest. Top-tier status (Marriott Titanium, Hilton Diamond, Hyatt Globalist) does produce reliable upgrade prioritization at properties that are part of those programs. But at independent luxury hotels, boutique properties, and non-branded resorts — which is most of the best accommodation in the world — points programs are irrelevant. The relational equivalent at those properties is an advisor relationship.

Length of stay is an underrated factor. A guest staying seven nights in a junior suite is more likely to receive a complimentary upgrade to a full suite than a guest staying two nights in the same category. The math is simple: the goodwill generated by a seven-night upgrade is higher, and the cost in foregone revenue is the same. If you're considering whether to add a night to your stay for other reasons, this is one more argument for doing so.

Arrival timing matters operationally. Upgrades are allocated in pre-arrival review, but they can also happen at check-in when a room isn't ready and the front desk needs to solve that problem. Late afternoon arrivals (4–6pm) often benefit from this dynamic: the hotel has had all day to manage inventory, rooms that weren't ready at 2pm are available now, and the front desk has more flexibility. Early arrivals (10am) create more pressure to get you settled quickly in whatever is ready — which is often the category you booked.

What doesn't work — and why

The pre-arrival email explaining that you're celebrating something is, at best, neutral. Hotels receive dozens of these. The staff processing them have no mechanism for acting on them — they can flag a note in the reservation, but that note goes into the same CRM field as the note from the anniversary couple in room 412 and the honeymoon couple in room 218. It doesn't create prioritization. It creates noise.

Asking for an upgrade at check-in is similarly unlikely to produce results at properties where you have no status or relationship. The front desk associate does not have discretionary authority to hand out upgrades to anyone who asks politely — those decisions were made in the morning meeting. What the associate can do is note the request for management. Whether management acts on it depends on inventory and their assessment of your value as a guest, which circles back to the same variables above.

Travel review sites do not produce upgrades. Mentioning that you write reviews, have followers, or will be posting about the property is the kind of thing that gets noted in the reservation file and occasionally produces results at smaller properties — but at serious luxury hotels, it reads as amateur and is more likely to make you memorable for the wrong reasons.

"Top-tier loyalty status works where the program covers. At independent luxury hotels — which is most of the best accommodation in the world — the relational equivalent is a recognized advisor booking."

Confirmed upgrades versus complimentary ones

There is an important distinction between a confirmed upgrade — which you have paid for, either in cash or points — and a complimentary upgrade, which the hotel provides at its discretion. Both are real. They function completely differently.

A confirmed upgrade is guaranteed: you booked a junior suite and you will receive a junior suite. A complimentary upgrade is at the hotel's discretion regardless of what you did to position for it. At the best properties, the distinction matters less because the baseline room is exceptional — the upgrade, when it comes, is a bonus rather than a rescue. At mid-tier properties, the baseline room can be poor enough that the upgrade matters a lot, and in those cases, it is worth spending the incremental amount to confirm the better category rather than gambling on the complimentary version.

The practical takeaway: if the room category genuinely matters to the trip — a specific view, a suite for a honeymoon, a room that justifies the property — book it confirmed. Don't rely on the complimentary system for something essential. Save the upgrade positioning for the situations where you'd be happy either way.

The one thing worth doing

If you want to increase your upgrade probability at a property you care about, the highest-leverage thing you can do is book through an advisor who has a recognized relationship with that property. The rest — status, length of stay, timing — is incremental. The channel through which you book is foundational, because it determines how you appear in the property's system before you ever arrive.

The second most useful thing: book a category you'll genuinely be happy in. The upgrade will be better than expected or it won't come at all — and either is fine, because the room you booked is already the right choice. Travelers who approach upgrades as a fallback for an underfunded booking are misusing the system and setting themselves up for disappointment. Upgrades go to guests who would have been happy without one.

How this works in practice

When Hit Your Mark Travel books a client into a property within our preferred network, the reservation arrives flagged and the client is known to the property before check-in. That means VIP arrivals notes, pre-arrival communication from management, and consistent upgrade consideration — not because we ask for it, but because the relationship has been built over time and the hotel wants to take care of clients who arrive through advisors they trust. This is one of the concrete, measurable ways the advisor relationship pays for itself on a trip.
