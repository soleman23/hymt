Why Active Travel

The Best Active Travel
Isn't the Most Extreme Version

Fitness requirements vary more here than any other planning input. A safari asks almost nothing physically; trekking to Machu Picchu or Everest Base Camp asks for months of cardiovascular preparation. Southern-hemisphere seasons run October to April and northern ones June to September, so there is good trekking somewhere in all 12 months. Current fitness sets the itinerary, not the reverse.

Active travel covers a wide range — from a week of moderate hiking in the Dolomites with rifugio dinners each night to a supported multi-day trek in Patagonia with gear transfers and a private guide. Neither is more valid than the other. What matters is the match: between your fitness level, your time, and what you're actually trying to get out of the trip.

The planning failures in active travel are specific. Permits that close before you book. Altitude acclimatization ignored in the itinerary. A guide with the wrong expertise for the terrain. A fitness level assumed rather than assessed. A twelve-mile hiking day scheduled before anyone's legs have adjusted. These aren't edge cases — they're the standard failures of active trips put together without someone who's actually done them.

We plan active trips with the same rigor we bring to a safari or a villa rental. The guide quality, the daily distances, the recovery days, the gear requirements, the contingency for weather — all of it is worked through before you land. The goal is a trip you finish feeling like you earned something, not one you endured.

Six Destinations Worth Moving Through

Each destination rewards physical engagement in a way that passive travel simply can't replicate. These are the places where the experience lives in the doing.

South America
Patagonia
The benchmark active destination — a place whose landscape is so singular that the only honest way to experience it is on foot. Torres del Paine's W Trek delivers five days of some of the most dramatic scenery on earth: the granite towers at dawn, the French Valley glacier lookout, the Grey Glacier at the lake's edge. Supported trekking with gear transfers and lodge accommodation makes it accessible to fit travelers without mountaineering experience. Plan twelve months out — refugio and hotel slots fill fast and permits are tightly managed.

Peru & the Andes
The Inca Trail to Machu Picchu is the most iconic active trip in South America — 26 miles, four days, two altitude passes above 13,000 feet, and a sunrise arrival at the Sun Gate that puts everything in perspective. Permits are limited to 500 people per day and sell out months in advance. The Salkantay and Lares alternatives are less crowded and equally rewarding for groups who miss the main trail window.

The Details

What Every Active Trip Includes

The planning work for an active trip is more involved than most, because the consequences of getting it wrong are more immediate.

⬡
Fitness Matching
We assess your current fitness level and experience against the demands of the itinerary before building anything. Daily distances, elevation gain, technical sections, and recovery days are all mapped to what you can actually do — not what the brochure assumes.
⬡
Permit & Slot Logistics
The Inca Trail, Milford Track, Torres del Paine refugios, and Iceland's Laugavegur all require permits or reservations that sell out months in advance. We secure these the moment dates are confirmed — not as an afterthought once the rest of the trip is built.
⬡
Guide Quality
A guide who knows a route technically is different from one who can read group energy, manage pace, and adapt the day when conditions shift. We source guides with both — and for high-altitude or technical terrain, we verify certifications and first-aid qualifications before placing anyone with them.
⬡
Gear & Preparation
A specific packing list for your exact itinerary, a training recommendation for the six weeks before you leave, and a weather briefing once your start date is within range. Not generic advice — specifics for the terrain, the season, and the number of days you'll be on the move.
When to Go

Active Travel Season by Destination

Southern Hemisphere Summer
October – April
The window for Patagonia, New Zealand, and Peru's high-altitude routes. Torres del Paine's W Trek is only reliably open October through April — outside that window, trails close and refugios shut down. New Zealand's Great Walks operate November through April. Book Patagonia accommodations and Peru permits twelve months in advance for this season.
Patagonia & New Zealand
Northern Hemisphere Summer
June – September
The primary window for European alpine trekking — Dolomites rifugi open mid-June through late September, Tour du Mont Blanc runs July through August, and Iceland's highland routes including the Laugavegur are accessible June through October. Iceland's midnight sun delivers twenty hours of usable daylight at peak summer, opening up day lengths no other active destination can match.
Year-Round Destinations
Any Month
Costa Rica operates year-round, with the dry season (December through April) offering more reliable conditions for rafting and coastal activities. Iceland is the strongest year-round active destination — winter adds ice cave access, Northern Lights on evening hikes, and glacier conditions unavailable in summer. Active travel in shoulder seasons often means better guide availability and smaller group sizes.

For diving, a PADI Open Water certification is required for most reef dives — courses are available at destination resorts if you don't have one. For trekking, technical training is rarely required unless you're pursuing true mountaineering. I brief clients clearly on exactly what preparation is needed and how much lead time is realistic.

I work exclusively with operators who have verifiable safety records, qualified guides, and proper rescue and emergency protocols. For higher-risk activities — high altitude trekking, backcountry expeditions — I also brief clients on specialist travel insurance requirements that include evacuation coverage.
