Why Multigenerational Travel

Three Generations.
Three Different Trips.
One Itinerary.

The planning problem is 3 sets of expectations at once, not a destination shortlist. What works is a property with enough variety that each generation gets something real, plus 1 or 2 shared anchors a day rather than a fully joint itinerary. European shoulder seasons, May–June and September–October, suit these groups best; school-holiday windows cost the most.

Grandparents want slow mornings, walkable cities, and dinner at a table that doesn't require standing in line. Parents want the trip to feel meaningful — some culture, some relaxation, some actual time away from logistics. Kids want to be engaged, not managed. Designing one itinerary that works across all three is not the same as compromising until nobody's happy.

The accommodation decision is almost always the most important one. A private villa — where three generations share a common space but retreat to their own rooms — changes the dynamic of the trip entirely. Everyone can move at their own pace, meals can happen on the group's schedule instead of a restaurant's, and there's no daily negotiation over what time to meet in the lobby.

Beyond accommodation, it's about building in optionality. The teenagers can take the cooking class while the grandparents sit in the garden. The parents can join either group, or take two hours alone. That kind of flexibility doesn't happen by accident — it requires a structure that anticipates the day when someone's energy doesn't match the group's plan, and makes room for it without derailing anything.

The Details

What Every Multigenerational Trip Includes

The planning that makes a multigenerational trip feel effortless is largely invisible by the time the group lands. Here's what goes into it.

⬡
Accommodation Configuration
Villa rentals, multi-bedroom resort suites, and connecting room blocks — sourced specifically for the number and makeup of your group. Private shared space is the single biggest factor in whether a multigenerational trip works. We prioritize it accordingly.
⬡
Pacing by Generation
Slow mornings for grandparents, activity windows for kids, and genuine downtime for parents. We build itineraries with optionality — days where the group can do different things and reconvene for dinner, without anyone feeling left out or left behind.
⬡
Accessibility Planning
Cobblestone streets, long walking days, and boat boarding steps are real considerations for older travelers. We assess accessibility at every point of the itinerary and flag limitations before they become problems — alternative routes, private transfers, and seated options where relevant.
⬡
Shared Experience Design
The memory that anchors a multigenerational trip is usually a single shared experience — a private boat day, a family dinner in a Tuscan villa, a ryokan evening in Kyoto. We identify and build those moments into every itinerary deliberately, not as an afterthought.
When to Go

Timing a Multigenerational Trip

European Shoulder
May – June & September – October
The ideal window for Italy, Portugal, Greece, and most of Europe. Warm enough for pools and beaches, cool enough for grandparents to walk comfortably. School calendars in this window vary — we can help navigate end-of-year and early-fall flexibility for families with students. Villa availability is best booked six to nine months out.
Best for Europe
School Holiday Windows
December – January & Summer
The primary booking windows when the whole family — including school-age children — can travel together. Winter break favors Caribbean and Mexico destinations with peak weather. Summer works for most destinations globally but requires earlier booking for villa and accommodation availability, particularly in Europe where private rentals fill by February.
Japan & Asia
March – May & October – November
Cherry blossom season in March and April is Japan's most requested window — book twelve months out for Kyoto. The fall foliage period in October and November is quieter, equally beautiful, and often more comfortable for older travelers. Both windows offer mild temperatures that work for grandparents who struggle in summer heat.

Common Questions

Multigenerational Travel — FAQ

Questions about planning trips for families spanning three generations — from accommodation logistics to managing different interests and physical abilities.

Pick a property with enough variety that each generation gets something real, then anchor each day with one or two shared experiences. A beach resort with a kids' club, adult spa, and accessible excursions, or a ranch property with both adventure and total relaxation options, can satisfy a group with dramatically different travel styles.

Villa rentals are the most flexible. Multiple bedrooms, shared common space, and a private environment where different generations can engage or retreat on their own schedule. Private lodge buyouts work similarly in certain destinations. Large suite configurations at resort hotels can accommodate groups of 8–12. I avoid putting multigenerational groups in standard hotel rooms that force everyone into the same schedule.

Costa Rica balances wildlife, adventure, and beach — accessible for grandparents, exciting for children, interesting for adults. Italy works across generations well: culture for grandparents, food and wine for adults, gelato and Roman ruins for kids. Ocean cruise ships, when chosen carefully, can accommodate three generations well. Caribbean and Mexico beach resorts remain the reliable default.

By building itineraries with optional intensity levels. Every day should have a high-activity option for those who want it and a comfortable, low-effort alternative for those who don't. Shared meals and certain experiences serve as anchors; individual activities within the day allow each traveler to choose their own engagement level without the group having to compromise constantly.

Groups of 8–25 are common for multigenerational trips. Beyond a certain size, logistics — transfers, restaurant reservations, room configurations — become more complex. I manage the full logistics for large groups and negotiate group rates with properties and operators. Planning lead time increases with group size; 12+ months is appropriate for peak season travel with groups over 15.

For groups of 10 or more, 9–12 months in advance is the right target for peak season travel. Finding properties with the right room configuration, securing a private villa, or booking a chartered yacht all require significant lead time. Starting early also allows gradual commitment from family members who need to coordinate schedules and travel budgets.

Trying to cover too much ground, which exhausts older travelers and bores younger ones. Choosing properties that work well for adults but poorly for children, or vice versa. Underestimating the complexity of coordinating a large group's logistics. And starting the planning too late, which limits options and increases cost. I've seen all of these and design specifically to avoid them.

I collect dietary information for every traveler as part of the planning process and communicate it to every property, guide, and restaurant on the itinerary. With a multigenerational group, there are often several different restrictions at once — allergies, religious dietary requirements, preference-based restrictions, and children's preferences. Managing this proactively removes the daily friction of figuring it out at each meal.
