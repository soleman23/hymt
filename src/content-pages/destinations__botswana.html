The Country

Low Volume. High Value.
The Botswana Model.

Botswana's dry season peaks June through October, when the Okavango flood is at its height and game concentrates on the water. Most safaris run 9–12 days across two or three camps. Botswana costs more than its neighbours by design: the government set a high minimum daily rate to cap visitor numbers, so the premium buys fewer vehicles at a sighting.

Botswana made a deliberate policy decision decades ago that still shapes every aspect of safari travel here: high-cost, low-volume tourism. Rather than maximizing visitor numbers, the government capped the number of guests permitted in each wilderness area, required most visitors to fly into private camps on light aircraft, and set the conservation fee structure to keep the ecosystem financially viable at low occupancy. The result is the most pristine safari experience available anywhere in Africa.

The Okavango Delta — a UNESCO World Heritage Site — is one of the natural world's great anomalies: the Okavango River flows inland from the Angolan highlands, spreads across the Kalahari Desert in a vast alluvial fan, and disappears into the sand without ever reaching the sea. The result is a permanent oasis in the middle of the world's second largest desert — a labyrinth of channels, lily-covered lagoons, and seasonal islands that support one of the highest concentrations of wildlife in Africa. Poling through it in a mokoro at dawn, in complete silence, is the experience that Botswana clients return home talking about twenty years later.

The elephant herds of Chobe are the largest in Africa — an estimated 130,000 animals in the Chobe ecosystem, more than any other country on earth. The Linyanti Concession holds one of the continent's highest concentrations of wild dogs. The Central Kalahari offers desert-adapted lions with the distinctive black manes of the Kalahari pride, in a reserve so vast that the nearest tourist vehicle may be thirty kilometers away. This is what Botswana charges for. It is worth it.

What We Add to Botswana Planning

Botswana is the destination where the camp selection matters most absolutely — the price range at the same "area" is enormous, and the difference in experience is proportional.

⬡
Concession vs National Park
Private concessions in Botswana — the Linyanti, Khwai, various Delta exclusives — operate under different rules than the national parks and Moremi Reserve. Night drives, off-road driving, bush walks, and fire access are permitted in concessions and prohibited in parks. The wildlife is the same; the experience is not. Knowing which camp sits on which land tenure and understanding what that means for your daily game drive is foundational planning knowledge that most booking platforms don't surface.
⬡
Seasonal Water Levels
The Okavango Delta's flooding peaks in July and August as the Angolan rains from four months earlier work their way down through the system. This transforms the accessible channels in specific areas — camps that are on islands in August may be reachable by vehicle in November. Knowing how the water levels affect specific camps in the year of travel, rather than relying on a generic seasonal calendar, is the planning work that separates a correctly positioned Delta trip from one that arrives at a camp in the wrong condition for its featured activity.
⬡
Camp Selection
Within any given Botswana area, the price range between the best and worst camp can be $500 per person per night while looking identical in photographs and marketing descriptions. The difference is in the guide-to-guest ratio, the condition of the equipment, the guide tenure and specialist qualifications, and the quality of the camp management. Our camp relationships and the debrief we run with every returning client tell us which camps within each area are worth their rate and which are trading on location rather than delivering on it.
⬡
Multi-Ecosystem Routing
Botswana's ecosystems are distinct enough that combining them in the wrong sequence — or spending too long in one area at the expense of another — undermines what makes the country exceptional. A three-camp circuit that moves logically between the Delta, Chobe, and either the Kalahari or Linyanti produces a trip that feels like three different countries. A circuit that sequences them incorrectly, or adds a fourth camp unnecessarily, produces pacing problems and diminishing returns. We build the routing around the specific season and the specific camps that perform best in that window.
When to Go

Botswana by Season

Dry Season Peak
June – October
The primary Botswana window. The dry season concentrates wildlife at permanent water sources — the Delta channels, the Chobe River, the Linyanti floodplain — and keeps the bush open and traversable. Delta flooding peaks in July and August, producing the best mokoro conditions of the year. The Chobe elephant aggregations are most dramatic in August through October as the dry season tightens. Game viewing across all Botswana ecosystems is at its best. Book six to nine months in advance for the best camps in this window.
Best Overall Window
Green Season
November – April
The rains transform Botswana — lush, green landscapes, dramatically lower rates (sometimes 30–40% below peak), and exceptional birdwatching as migratory species arrive. The Makgadikgadi Pans fill with water and the zebra migration arrives in December and January. Newborn wildlife is abundant. The Kalahari is at its most photogenic in green season, with the pans reflecting sky and the predators focused on the migrating herds. Not the right choice for travelers who prioritize concentrated game viewing, but genuinely excellent for the right traveler.
Shoulder Season
April – June
April and May are the transition out of the wet season — the Delta flooding is building, the bush is still green, and the rates are slightly below peak. Some camps are closed in April for maintenance. June marks the start of the dry season proper — the best time to see the Delta beginning to concentrate wildlife on its islands as the water around them recedes. For travelers with flexibility in timing, late May or early June offers a genuinely excellent Botswana experience at below-peak cost with fewer other guests.
A Note from Mark

On Botswana vs East Africa

Mark Sole
Founder & Travel Director · Hit Your Mark Travel

"I recommend Botswana to travelers who have already done Kenya and Tanzania and want to understand what safari can be when there are genuinely no compromises made. The comparison is direct: Kenya's conservancies are excellent precisely because they limit vehicles and guests — they are, in effect, trying to approximate what Botswana does by policy. Botswana does it at a national level. When you are in the Okavango Delta in a mokoro in July, there is no sound. There is no vehicle. There is no evidence that the modern world exists. The camp you leave every morning is as good as anything in East Africa, but the experience on the water has no equivalent there. Is it more expensive? Yes. Is that cost justified? In my opinion, unambiguously. The number of clients I have sent to Botswana who found it underwhelming is zero."

Common Questions

Botswana — FAQ

Questions we hear most when planning Botswana travel — from the cost conversation to mokoro versus motorboat, malaria zones, and how to combine Botswana with neighboring countries.

Botswana's government set a high minimum daily rate for tourism in wilderness areas specifically to limit visitor numbers and fund conservation through the cost structure. Camps pay significant concession fees and are permitted to operate only if they maintain the infrastructure and guide quality the policy demands. The result is that the expensive camps in Botswana are expensive because the model requires it — and the experience those rates buy has no equivalent at a lower price point anywhere in Africa. The cost is real. So is the justification.

The Okavango Delta, Chobe, and the Linyanti are all malaria zones. The Central Kalahari is considered lower risk but not malaria-free. Prophylaxis is recommended for all safari areas. There are no significant malaria-free game reserves in Botswana comparable to South Africa's Sabi Sands — this is not a destination for travelers who need to avoid malaria zones entirely. Camps take precautions seriously: treated nets, evening spray, long-sleeve recommendations after dark.

A mokoro is a traditional dugout canoe poled silently through the Delta channels. You sit at water level, in complete silence, and the activity focuses on the birdlife, the water lilies, the smaller mammals, and the extraordinary atmosphere of the waterway. It is not a game-drive format; large predators are not the focus. A motorboat covers more ground and allows access to hippo pools and crocodile banks. Most Delta camps offer both, and a good stay typically includes both activities on different days. Neither replaces the other.

Tanzania (specifically the Serengeti circuit) is the more common starting point — lower total cost, better-known destination, the migration spectacle. Botswana is better positioned as a second or third Africa trip for travelers who want to understand what exclusive safari means after they've experienced the iconic version. That said, some travelers prefer to start at the top — and for them, Botswana first makes complete sense. The conversation about which to prioritize is worth having before booking either.

Wild dog denning season runs approximately May through August. The pack is anchored to the den site, which means predictable daily sightings as the adults return to feed the pups. The Linyanti Concession and the Khwai area have resident packs. Outside denning season, wild dogs range widely and sightings are less predictable. If seeing wild dogs is a priority, May through August in the Linyanti or Khwai is the specific recommendation, and camps with known resident packs are the ones worth booking.

Two camps is sufficient for a very strong Botswana trip — typically Delta and Chobe, or Delta and Linyanti. Three camps allows the inclusion of a third ecosystem (Kalahari, Makgadikgadi) and gives each area more than two or three nights. The sweet spot for most travelers is nine to twelve days across two or three camps, with at least three nights in the Delta. Fewer than three nights in the Delta tends to feel rushed given the amount of time spent transferring by light aircraft to get there.

Most Botswana camps are accessible only by light aircraft — typically a Cessna Caravan or similar. Flights operate from Maun (the Delta gateway) or Kasane (for Chobe) to a bush airstrip near the camp, usually a fifteen to forty-five minute flight. The weight restriction on luggage is real: most camps restrict to fifteen kilograms of soft-sided bags per person. This is a genuine planning consideration — hard suitcases don't work. We brief clients fully on the logistics and what to pack for a fly-in circuit.

Adding two nights at the Falls — on either side of the border — converts a Botswana-only trip into the most compelling Southern Africa combination available. South Luangwa in Zambia pairs well with Botswana for travelers who want walking safaris as a complement to the Botswana vehicle and mokoro model.
