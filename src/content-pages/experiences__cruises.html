Why Cruise Travel

Small Ships, Real Itineraries

Ship size decides the experience more than the itinerary does. A 200-guest expedition ship means no queuing and a destination focus; a large ship means more amenities and more people between you and the place. Mediterranean shoulder seasons run May–June and September–October, Alaska June to August, and Antarctica November to March. Expedition cabins book twelve to eighteen months out.

The cruise industry is built around a volume model: five thousand passengers, a port every day, and an onboard economy designed to keep you spending. That model produces a specific kind of trip — and it's rarely the one our clients are looking for.

The version of cruising we plan is different. Ships measured in hundreds of guests, not thousands. Itineraries that stay late in port or overnight where the day-trippers leave. Expedition vessels with naturalists and Zodiacs where the destination is the point, river ships that dock in the center of the city you're there to see, and private yachts where the itinerary is a conversation, not a schedule.

The choice of vessel, cabin category, and sailing date matters more in cruising than in almost any other travel category — the same itinerary on two different ships is two different trips. That matching problem is what we're for.

The Details

What Every Cruise Booking Includes

The fare is the beginning, not the end. What surrounds it is where trips are made or quietly diminished.

⬡
Ship & Cabin Selection
We vet the vessels we recommend deck plan by deck plan, with the cruise lines and with clients who have just sailed them. We know which cabin categories justify their premium, which decks to avoid, and which 'suites' are marketing. On the right ship, in the right cabin, the same itinerary is a different trip.
⬡
Pre & Post Extensions
The cruise is rarely the whole trip. We build the land portion around it — the two nights in Athens before the Aegean, the wine country week after the Douro — so the journey has a beginning and an end, not just a middle.
⬡
Private Shore Arrangements
Ship excursions move at the speed of the slowest passenger. We arrange private guides and drivers in the ports that matter — you see the place on your schedule, with someone who knows it, and you're back before the gangway closes.
⬡
Expedition Vetting
For polar, Galápagos, and remote expedition sailings, the operator's safety record, naturalist quality, and landing logistics are the trip. We book expedition lines with serious track records — and we can tell you why, specifically.
⬡
Charter Brokerage
For private yachts, we work with established charter brokers, inspect crew references, and structure the provisioning around your table — not a standard menu. The boat is the easy part; the crew is everything.
⬡
Flights, Transfers & the Boring Parts
Embarkation day has a way of going wrong in small ways. Flights timed to the sailing, transfers that know the terminal, and a plan for the luggage — all confirmed before you leave home.

Cruising Season by Region

The same coastline in the wrong month is a different trip. Timing is the first decision.

Mediterranean Shoulder
May – June · Sept – Oct
Warm water, working harbors, and restaurant tables you can actually get. The Med at its best — before and after the August compression.
Best Window
Alaska & Expedition North
June – August
The short northern window. Wildlife at peak activity, the longest days, and expedition ships deployed to Alaska, Greenland, and the High Arctic.
Antarctica & the South
November – March
The austral summer is the only window. November for pristine snow and penguin courtship; February–March for whales and the warmest temperatures.

It depends entirely on the ship. Clients who swear they 'aren't cruise people' often love small-ship and expedition cruising — two hundred guests, a destination focus, no queueing. What they dislike is the volume model, not the water. The first conversation is about what you want the trip to feel like; the vessel follows from that.

Everything that matters. Small ships dock in harbors big ships can't enter, stay in port after the day-trippers leave, and run dinner as a restaurant rather than a seating. Big ships offer more onboard entertainment and lower headline fares — but the destination becomes a backdrop. If the point is the place, the ship should be small.

Twelve to eighteen months ahead for Antarctica, the Galápagos, and High Arctic sailings. The best ships carry one to two hundred guests, and the desirable cabin categories sell out long before departure. Last-minute expedition deals exist, but they're rarely on the ships we'd put you on.
