Why Family Travel

Family Travel Isn't
Adult Travel with Kids Added

Most luxury safari camps in Botswana and Tanzania set a minimum age of 12, and that single constraint reshapes more family itineraries than any other. South Africa, Kenya and certain family-designated camps take younger children. The school-holiday windows — December–January and summer — carry the highest rates and the earliest sell-outs of the year, so they set the booking clock.

The most common family travel mistake is planning an adult trip and expecting the kids to adapt. It rarely works. The itinerary that's perfect for two adults becomes exhausting for a family of four — too many transitions, too much time in transit, too little margin for the day when someone's tired and nobody's hungry at the same time.

Family travel requires different questions. Is the beach entry shallow enough for a five-year-old? Do the connecting rooms actually connect, or is "adjoining" a loose term here? Is the kids' program genuinely engaging or a holding area with cartoons? Which half of the day should be activity and which should be unstructured? What happens when the weather turns and the beach plan falls apart?

We plan around how families actually travel — with flexibility built in, age-appropriate pacing, and a complete picture of the property before you land. The trips we put together for families run smoothly not because everything goes perfectly, but because we've accounted for what doesn't.

The Details

What Every Family Trip Includes

Family travel has more moving parts than any other trip type. Here's how we handle them.

⬡
Age-Appropriate Planning
What works for a 4-year-old is different from what works for a 12-year-old. We build itineraries around the actual ages in your group — not a generic "family-friendly" template that fits nobody particularly well.
⬡
Room Configuration
Connecting rooms, family suites, multiple-bedroom villa options — we verify actual room configurations before booking, not just what the property website implies. "Adjoining" means different things at different resorts.
⬡
Kids' Programming Vetting
We know which kids' clubs are genuinely well-run and which are marketing copy. Age-group structure, staff ratios, activity quality, and whether parents actually get a few hours to themselves — all assessed before we recommend a property.
⬡
Pacing & Logistics
Family itineraries need built-in flexibility — slower mornings, unscheduled afternoons, buffer days. We build margin into every trip so that when someone's tired or the weather turns, the whole plan doesn't collapse.
When to Go

Family Travel Season by Season

Winter Break
December – January
The most in-demand family travel window. Peak season for Caribbean and Mexico coincides perfectly with school holidays — dry weather, calm water, and the trips parents book a year in advance. Top properties fill fast. Book at minimum six months out for the last two weeks of December.
Book Earliest
Summer
June – August
The dominant family travel season by volume. Mexico and the Caribbean are hot and humid but largely functional. Europe is excellent in June before the peak crowds arrive. Hawaii is ideal year-round but books early in summer. Advance planning of four to six months is standard for peak July and August travel.
Spring Break & Shoulder
March – April, October – November
The most underutilized family travel windows. Spring break in Mexico and the Caribbean has excellent weather and strong property availability compared to December. Fall shoulder offers meaningfully lower rates and quieter resorts — particularly valuable for families flexible on school calendar or homeschooling.

Most luxury safari camps in Botswana and Tanzania set a minimum age of 12. This reflects the unpredictability of game drives and the need for quiet in the bush. South Africa, Kenya, and certain family-specific camps accommodate children as young as 5 or 6 with dedicated programs and appropriate vehicles. I look for properties where children's programming is substantive rather than afterthought. Dedicated staff, age-appropriate activities, and separate facilities that let parents relax while children are genuinely engaged. I also assess beach safety, food quality for children, and whether the property's overall ambiance is family-oriented or primarily couples-focused.

Caribbean and Mexico all-inclusive resorts, certain European beach destinations (Sardinia, the Algarve, Majorca), and properties in Bali and the Maldives all have strong family infrastructure. What matters most is property selection — a well-run resort in any of these regions can deliver an extraordinary family experience regardless of destination.

The structure of the itinerary matters as much as the destination. I look for properties and destinations with enough variety to give each age group something meaningful — kids have programming and activities, adults have genuine time for themselves, and shared experiences anchor the trip. Shared meals, excursions, and a few activities everyone can do together are the backbone.

For families, I'd say travel insurance is more important than for any other traveler type. The reasons are straightforward: children can get sick unexpectedly, school schedules limit flexibility for rebooking, and the financial exposure of a family cancellation is larger than a solo traveler's. I recommend policies that cover trip cancellation, interruption, and medical evacuation as a minimum.
