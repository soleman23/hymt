The Country

Depth Beats Breadth.
Always in Japan.

The concept of shun — the Japanese idea that each ingredient has a peak season measured in weeks, sometimes days, and that eating it outside that window is a lesser version of the thing — applies to Japan as a destination as precisely as it applies to matsutake mushrooms in October. Cherry blossom is a specific two-week window that moves north across the country from late March. The autumn foliage peaks in Kyoto's temple gardens in mid-November, not all November. The fish market at Tsukiji produces its best tuna in the cold months of January and February. Planning Japan around shun, in the broadest sense, is what separates a beautiful trip from an exceptional one.

The ryokan tradition — traditional Japanese inns with tatami mat rooms, futon beds, multi-course kaiseki dinners, and shared or private onsen baths — represents a philosophy of hospitality that is unlike anything available in Western luxury hotels. The best ryokan have been operating for generations under the same families, and the service model reflects an understanding of anticipatory hospitality that the hotel industry has not been able to replicate. Selecting the right ryokan for the right night of a Japan itinerary is one of the most consequential planning decisions we make.

The specialist guide makes the difference between knowing that the Fushimi Inari Shrine has ten thousand torii gates and understanding why they were built, who paid for them, and what the mountain behind them means within the Shinto cosmology that shaped the city around it. Japan rewards the specialist absolutely and punishes the generalist with pleasant but superficial experiences that leave the country feeling like a series of sights rather than a coherent civilization.

Kansai Region
Osaka
Kuromon Ichiba Market at mid-morning when the wholesale sellers are transitioning to retail and the prepared food stalls are firing up — grilled wagyu, fresh oysters, fruit on sticks that have no equivalent elsewhere. Dotonbori in the late evening when the neon reflects off the canal and the takoyaki vendors are three deep at the counter. Osaka has a justified reputation as the culinary counterpoint to Tokyo's refinement: louder, more generous with portions, less interested in presentation and more in flavor. Hiroshima & Miyajima

Destination Photography · Kanazawa
Chubu Region
Kanazawa
Kanazawa is what Kyoto was before the tourist infrastructure arrived and transformed it into a destination. Kenroku-en garden — one of Japan's three great landscape gardens — is still walkable in early morning without crowds. The Higashi Chaya geisha district has ochaya teahouses that still function as performance venues rather than tourist sites. The Omicho Market is the working food market that serves the city's restaurants. Kutani ceramics craftsmen in the Higashi Chaya area still produce the distinctive overglazed Kanazawa ware in studios that have been operating for three hundred years. Two nights here adds the Japan that most itineraries sacrifice for a third night in Kyoto.

Rural Japan
Shirakawa-go's gassho-zukuri farmhouses, whose steep thatched roofs are designed to shed the Gifu Prefecture snowfall that reaches two meters in a heavy winter, are a UNESCO World Heritage Site that most itineraries schedule as a day trip from Kanazawa. Staying overnight — in one of the farmhouses converted to a minshuku inn — produces an entirely different encounter with the village. Hakone for the onsen and the Fuji views. Hakuba for the Japan Alps in spring when the snow is still on the ridges. Yakushima for the cedar forests of Jomon Sugi, trees that have been growing for two thousand years on an island that receives ten meters of rainfall annually.

We know which properties in each region are operating with genuine intent and which are trading on the category name. Crowd Avoidance
Fushimi Inari Shrine has ten thousand torii gates and, at 10am in April, has approximately that many visitors. At 5:30am, it has you, one photographer, and the sound of the wooden gates in the early morning breeze. Arashiyama Bamboo Grove is the same ratio between dawn and mid-morning. Knowing the specific times, the specific entry points, and the specific days of week when the crowd dynamics at Japan's most visited sites shift from overwhelming to extraordinary is operational planning knowledge that changes the experience materially.
When to Go

Japan by Season

Cherry Blossom Window
Late March – Early April
The most requested Japan window — and the one requiring the most lead time for accommodation (twelve months for the best ryokan, six months minimum for good hotels). The exact dates vary by up to two weeks annually based on winter temperature. Tokyo typically peaks late March to early April; Kyoto follows by three to five days; Hirosaki in the north runs late April. We track the Japan Meteorological Corporation forecast and help clients build flexible dates where possible. The crowds are real; the experience, with the right morning timing, is genuinely extraordinary.
Most Requested Window
Autumn Foliage
October – November
Japan's second most beautiful season — and, in our assessment, the better travel window for most clients. The foliage peaks mid-October in the north and progresses south through November, reaching Kyoto at its peak in mid-November. Crisp air, shorter days that compress the golden light, and autumn food culture (matsutake, kuri, kabocha) at its seasonal best. The crowds are smaller than cherry blossom season, accommodation is slightly easier to book, and the combination of foliage and autumn cuisine is as compelling as the blossom. For first-time travelers who can be flexible on season, this is frequently what we recommend.
Other Seasons
June–September & December–February
June and July are tsuyu — the rainy season — which dampens spirits and photography in equal measure; Kyoto in June rain has its own beauty but is not what most clients imagine. August is summer festival season (matsuri) with genuine cultural depth if the heat (35°C) is manageable. Winter — December through February — brings snow to Kyoto's temple gardens and Kanazawa, ski season in Hakuba, and the best omakase tuna season in Tokyo. The crowds are smallest in winter and accommodation rates are at their annual low. A genuinely excellent Japan option for the right traveler.

Common Questions

Japan — FAQ

Questions we hear most when planning Japan — from timing cherry blossom to ryokan selection, city sequencing, and dietary accommodation for non-Japanese speakers.

When is the best time to visit Japan?+
Cherry blossom (late March–early April) and autumn foliage (mid-October through November) are the two peak seasons, each with genuine justification. Cherry blossom requires the most advance planning and has the largest crowds; autumn has the better food season and, in our view, the more complete travel experience. Spring is the more iconic Japan image; autumn is the one more clients prefer in retrospect. Winter is excellent for ryokan and ski culture with minimal crowds. June through September is the least recommended for most travelers — hot, humid, with tsuyu rains in June and July.
Is the language barrier a serious issue in Japan?+
Less than most travelers expect at the practical level — the train system has English signage, convenience stores operate with point-and-pay simplicity, and Google Translate's camera function handles most menus in seconds. At the experiential level, the language barrier is significant: the authentic ramen shop, the sake bar with no English menu, the craft ceramics studio in a back alley — these experiences require either a guide or considerable comfort with navigating Japan in Japanese. This is where the specialist guide pays back far more than the cost.
What is a ryokan, and how do I choose one?+
A ryokan is a traditional Japanese inn characterized by tatami mat rooms, futon sleeping arrangements, yukata robes, multi-course kaiseki dinners, and onsen (hot spring) bathing facilities — shared or private. The experience ranges from extraordinary (family-run inns operating for generations with genuine omotenashi hospitality) to disappointing (commercial operations with tatami floors and mediocre food). The right ryokan for a given traveler depends on whether they want shared or private onsen, their comfort with communal spaces, and their appetite for the full kaiseki dinner format. We match travelers to the right property.
Is the JR Pass worth it, or should I buy individual tickets?+
For a standard Tokyo-Kyoto-Osaka circuit, the JR Pass (currently around $400 for seven days) is approximately break-even with buying individual Shinkansen tickets. It becomes clearly valuable if you add Hiroshima, Kanazawa, or northern Japan destinations. The pass also covers the Narita Express airport transfer. The calculation changes annually with JR pricing; we provide a specific cost comparison as part of every Japan itinerary. The pass must be purchased before arriving in Japan, which adds a planning step that catches some travelers off guard.
Should Tokyo come first or last in a Japan itinerary?+
Tokyo first is the more common routing and has the practical advantage of the direct Narita Express connection and acclimatization before the more culturally demanding Kyoto experience. Tokyo last allows departure from Osaka (connected to Kansai International) with the Kyoto and Osaka experiences still fresh, and some travelers find the energy and scale of Tokyo a better re-entry point to the modern world before flying home. Neither is wrong. We build the routing around which airport is cheaper to fly into and which experiences the client is most anticipating.
How many cities should a first-time Japan traveler try to see?+
Three cities in twelve days is the sweet spot: Tokyo (four nights), Kyoto (four nights), and Osaka (two nights), with a Hiroshima day trip added from Osaka or Kyoto. Adding more cities — Kanazawa, Nara, Nikko — before the three-city circuit is properly explored tends to produce a trip that feels like a highlight reel rather than a genuine encounter with any of them. Japan rewards time in each place more than breadth across many. The exception is Kanazawa, which is best added to a second Japan trip for the traveler who already knows Tokyo and Kyoto well.
How far in advance do I need to book for cherry blossom season?+
Twelve months for the best ryokan; nine months for quality hotels in Tokyo and Kyoto; six months as an absolute minimum for anything that doesn't involve two-star business hotels. Cherry blossom timing cannot be confirmed until six to eight weeks before the peak, which creates an uncomfortable planning situation — you must book accommodation a year in advance for dates that may be one to two weeks off the actual blossom peak. We help clients build itinerary flexibility where possible and track forecasts to adjust the sequence if timing allows.
How is Japan for vegetarians and special dietary needs?+
Japan presents specific challenges for strict vegetarians — dashi stock (made from bonito fish flakes) is foundational to most Japanese cooking, including many dishes described as "vegetable" in translation. Vegan travelers face significant menu limitations outside urban areas. Buddhist temple cuisine (shojin ryori) is inherently vegetarian and available at some Kyoto temples and specialized restaurants. We communicate dietary requirements in advance in Japanese for every restaurant reservation, and we avoid sending strict vegetarians to omakase counters where the menu cannot be modified. With advance planning, Japan is manageable; without it, it's difficult.
