The Voyage

South Georgia Is Why
You Take the Long Version.

The Falklands and South Georgia are what turn a 10-day Antarctic voyage into an 18–22 day one. The season runs October to March. South Georgia's king penguin beaches hold birds in the hundreds of thousands, and every one of those beaches is reached by Zodiac and cancelled by weather. The Falklands also work as a separate land trip, reached by scheduled air service.

Most people who book Antarctica mean the Peninsula: ten to twelve days out of Ushuaia, two Drake crossings, daily landings among gentoo and chinstrap colonies. That trip is set out on the Antarctica planning page, and for a first polar voyage it is usually the right one. This page is about the other version — the eighteen to twenty-two day itinerary that adds the Falklands and South Georgia before the ice. It costs another week and a great deal more money, and the people who have done both rarely name the Peninsula as the part they still talk about.

South Georgia is the reason. It is a mountain range with beaches attached, roughly 170 km long, rising to Mount Paget at 2,934 m, and it has no airstrip, no hotel and no road — every visitor arrives by ship and steps ashore from a Zodiac. Its beaches carry king penguin colonies counted in the hundreds of thousands of birds, most of the world's Antarctic fur seals, and wandering albatross nesting on offshore islets. Every visit is managed under a published visitor plan by the Government of South Georgia and the South Sandwich Islands.

The Falklands are a different proposition and deserve to be separated from the ship. They are an inhabited archipelago of more than 700 islands with a resident population of roughly 3,700, scheduled air links, farm lodges on the outer islands and a government air service flying between them. Five penguin species breed there, along with around 70% of the world's black-browed albatross, according to Falklands Conservation. You can go for a week without ever boarding an expedition vessel, for a fraction of what a voyage costs.

Six Landings Worth the Voyage

South Georgia carries most of this itinerary and the Falklands carry the front of it. Every site below is a Zodiac landing, subject to swell, wind and a cap on people ashore. No voyage gets all six.

South Georgia
St Andrews Bay
The largest king penguin colony on the island and the landing every other one is measured against. Birds spread across a glacial outwash plain in the hundreds of thousands, with elephant seals hauled out in front of them. Surf on this beach cancels more landings than any other site on the route.

Grytviken & Cumberland Bay
The only settlement, the only port of entry, and the single landing that is about people rather than animals. The whaling station, the museum, the white church, and Shackleton's grave in the whalers' cemetery where he was buried in 1922. It is also the only post office on the island.

Gold Harbour & Drygalski Fjord
The south-east end, and usually the last morning before the ship turns for the Peninsula. Gold Harbour compresses kings, elephant seals and a hanging glacier into one small amphitheatre of a beach. Stanley & Volunteer Point
The capital, and the king penguin colony you reach by land instead of by ship. Volunteer Point is roughly two hours from Stanley in a 4x4, most of it off-road across open camp, and it is the only mainland king colony in the Falklands. Book the vehicle and the driver, not a bus seat.

Featured Itineraries

Trips We Plan Often

Four shapes, and the choice between them is really a choice about days and about whether the ice matters more to you than the birds.

October – March · 18–22 Days
The Full Southern Ocean Circuit
Ushuaia to the Falklands first, for two days of landings that work as an introduction rather than a highlight — gentoos in the tussock, rockhoppers on the cliffs, and the first black-browed albatross most people have stood near. Then two days at sea to South Georgia and four or five days working the north coast: Salisbury Plain, Grytviken, St Andrews Bay, Gold Harbour, and whatever the weather allows in between. Two more sea days to the South Orkneys and the Peninsula for six or seven days of ice, then the Drake home. It is a long time on a ship and it is the itinerary experienced polar travellers rate above every other.

South Georgia Without the Peninsula
The itinerary almost nobody asks for and the one I recommend most often to people who have already been to the Peninsula. November – February · 8–10 Days
The Falklands by Air, No Ship
A land trip, and by a wide margin the cheapest way to stand in a penguin colony in the South Atlantic. Two nights in Stanley with a 4x4 day out to Volunteer Point for the king colony, then the government air service out to two outer islands for three nights each — Carcass or Pebble for the passerines and the beaches, Saunders or Sea Lion for the rockhopper cliffs and the black-browed albatross. Farm lodges, full board, no formal programme and no ship's tannoy. Aircraft are small, seats are limited and baggage allowances are strict, so island order is booked well ahead.

February – March · 18–21 Days
Late Season, for Whales and Albatross
The same circuit taken at the far end of the season, for a specific set of reasons. Humpback and fin whales are feeding in numbers off South Georgia and in the Peninsula channels by February. Fur seal pups have dispersed off the landing beaches, which reopens sites that were unusable in December. Prion Island is open again after its pupping closure, so the wandering albatross boardwalk goes back on the table. King penguin chicks are in full brown down and unmissable. The trade is colder, darker evenings and a rougher average sea state — take it if wildlife, not weather, is what you are optimising for.

Why Plan With Us

What We Add to South Georgia Planning

This is a trip where the decisions made at booking determine how many hours you spend ashore, and almost none of them are visible in a brochure.

⬡
Passenger Count Is Landing Time
Under IAATO operating procedures no more than 100 passengers are ashore at one site at any moment, and vessels carrying over 500 passengers do not land at all. On a 100-berth ship everyone is ashore for the whole window. On a 200-berth ship the same window is split in two and your morning at St Andrews Bay halves. That single number decides more about this voyage than the cabin, the food or the brand on the hull, and it is the first thing we look at.
⬡
Landings Are Weather, Not Itinerary
Every published South Georgia itinerary is a list of intentions. Katabatic wind off the icefields and swell running onto open beaches cancel landings routinely, and St Andrews Bay in particular is lost to surf more often than operators advertise. The honest planning response is not a better brochure but more days at the island: five gives you slack, three does not. We also build the trip so a lost morning is disappointing rather than ruinous, which mostly means not hanging the whole voyage on one site.
⬡
Biosecurity Is Part of the Day
South Georgia was cleared of rats, mice and reindeer over a decade of work, and the South Georgia Heritage Trust declared the island rodent-free in 2018. Keeping it that way is why every visitor vacuums pockets, seams and Velcro, scrubs boot treads and washes tripods and camera bags before the first landing and between sites. It takes real hours out of a sea day and nobody mentions it before you board. Pack for it: fewer Velcro closures, no field kit that has been in grassland since it was last cleaned.
⬡
The Falklands Half Nobody Plans
On a voyage the Falklands get a day and a half and are treated as a warm-up. As a standalone trip they are a genuine birding destination with lodges, and the two versions barely overlap. The land version runs on the government air service, which flies small aircraft on schedules set the day before, with strict baggage limits and limited seats. That is a planning problem, not a booking one, and it is the reason most people who intended to add the outer islands end up seeing only Stanley. Lodges are small, often a single house taking eight or ten guests, and in December and January they are committed a year out. We treat the island order and the flights as the fixed points and fit the rest around them.
When to Go

South Georgia by Season

Early Season
October – November
The strongest window if South Georgia rather than the ice is the point. Snow still lies on the beaches and behind the colonies, which is what makes the photographs from this end of the season look different from everyone else's. Elephant seals are still on the breeding beaches, king colonies hold last season's brown-downed chicks alongside new eggs, and the fur seals that later carpet the landing sites have not yet arrived in force. Prion Island's albatross boardwalk is open until its pupping closure on 20 November. Fewer ships are running, and the Peninsula end of a combined voyage is colder and icier.
Best for South Georgia
High Summer
December – January
The longest days, the warmest air and the busiest departures. It is the easiest window for a first polar trip and the hardest one for South Georgia landings: fur seal pupping peaks in early December, and territorial bulls make some beaches genuinely unworkable for weeks. Prion Island closes from 20 November to 7 January for that reason. Penguin chicks are growing, the light runs almost around the clock, and the Peninsula half of a combined voyage is at its most reliable. If you are going once and want everything to be as straightforward as it can be, this is the window — book it eighteen months out.
Late Season
February – March
Whale season, and the quietest end of the calendar. Humpback and fin whales are feeding off South Georgia and through the Peninsula channels, in numbers that late-season travellers talk about for years afterwards. Fur seal pups have moved off the beaches, so landing sites that were blocked in December come back. Prion Island is open again. King penguin chicks are in full brown down, and the colonies are at their loudest. Against that: shorter days, a colder ship, and an average sea state that is worse than December. Departures are fewer and the ships are less full.

Less demanding than it sounds, though Zodiac transfers and wet beach landings need reasonable balance and steady footing on loose ground. Landings are walks, not hikes. What the trip genuinely asks for is tolerance: three weeks on a small ship, four or more open-ocean sea days, and a schedule that changes daily. It is the wrong trip for anyone who needs a fixed plan, for a short holiday window, or for a first-time polar traveller on a tight budget. It is the right one for people who will happily spend six hours a day on a beach in the wind.
