There are roughly 50,000 five-star hotels in the world, depending on whose taxonomy you use. The number itself tells you what you need to know about the category. A rating system designed to identify the finest accommodation available has been applied so widely and inconsistently that it now describes a range spanning from genuinely transformative properties to large-chain business hotels with a spa and a lobby bar.

This is not a cynical observation. It is a structural problem with the rating framework itself. There is no single global authority for hotel star ratings. Different countries use different systems. Forbes Travel Guide uses one methodology; the AAA Diamond system uses another; national tourism boards have their own criteria; many properties self-classify. A five-star in Dubai operates under UAE standards. A five-star in Italy may have been rated by the regional tourism authority against criteria last updated in the 1990s. These are not equivalent things, and treating them as equivalent leads directly to expensive disappointment.

"The rating system was designed to help travelers identify exceptional hotels. Instead, it has become a minimum standard that exceptional hotels share with a very large number of unremarkable ones."

How the inflation happened

The expansion of the five-star category is primarily a supply problem. International luxury hotel development accelerated dramatically from the 1990s onward — every major brand extended its portfolio, new markets opened (the Gulf, Southeast Asia, China), and properties that met a particular checklist of physical amenities qualified for the highest available classification. Pool: check. Spa: check. Multiple restaurants: check. 24-hour room service: check. Business center: check. Five stars.

The checklist approach creates a category defined by inputs rather than outputs — what the property has rather than what it produces. A hotel can have every amenity on the list and still deliver a mediocre experience: indifferent service, generic food, architecture that could belong anywhere, a location chosen for convention center proximity rather than anything worth looking at. The rating doesn't distinguish between these things because the rating was never designed to measure them.

The Forbes Travel Guide star system — one of the more rigorous independent frameworks — has long been understood by industry insiders as the more meaningful designator, because Forbes conducts anonymous inspections against specific service criteria rather than relying on property self-reporting. A Forbes five-star is a substantially different claim than a government-assigned five-star. Most travelers don't know this distinction exists, and the properties that hold it rarely lead with the credential because it requires an explanation most guests haven't asked for.

What the category can't tell you

The five-star rating, even at its most rigorous, cannot tell you several things that are essential to whether a property will be right for a particular trip.

It can't tell you whether the property fits the destination. A property in Tuscany that opened in a renovated 15th-century convent with original frescoes and a walled garden is five stars. A property in the same region that opened as a purpose-built resort hotel with identical room layouts across six floors is also five stars. These are not the same thing. The rating doesn't care whether the property is intrinsic to its setting or interchangeable with any other.

It can't tell you the actual service quality. Service is the hardest thing to assess without being there, and it is arguably the most important variable at the high end. The gap between a hotel that is operationally proficient and one where the staff have been selected and trained toward genuine hospitality — where interactions feel like care rather than procedure — is enormous and invisible to any rating system. Some of the most memorable hotel stays I have had were at properties carrying no particularly prestigious certification. Some of the most disappointing were at properties with impeccable credentials.

It can't tell you about the guest mix. A 500-room five-star in a major convention city will have a different guest population, noise level, and service ratio than a 20-suite boutique property in the same classification. These produce categorically different experiences. The rating applies equally to both.

It can't tell you whether recent management or ownership changes have degraded the experience. Properties change. A hotel that earned its reputation five years ago may have reduced staffing ratios, changed chefs, deferred maintenance, or altered the character of the experience in ways that no rating system updates in real time.

What actually tells you something useful

The frameworks that provide genuinely useful signal have moved away from single-number ratings toward more granular assessments. The Leading Hotels of the World membership is a meaningful indicator — the collection is curated and managed, and properties are subject to ongoing quality review. Relais & Châteaux operates with explicit selection criteria around character, cuisine, and personality that the star system ignores. Preferred Hotels & Resorts L.V.X. collection maintains standards that carry specific meaning within the luxury segment.

Within the safari and expedition market, the ratings are almost entirely meaningless. Singita, &Beyond, Wilderness Safaris, and Asilia — the names that represent genuine quality in this segment — operate outside the hotel star framework entirely. What tells you something there is tenure: how long the company has been operating in the relevant ecosystem, guide training standards, vehicle-to-guest ratios, and the concession model (private versus shared).

Traveler reviews are useful in aggregate for identifying outliers — properties that consistently disappoint despite strong credentials, or genuinely exceptional smaller properties that haven't bothered with formal classification. But reviews skew toward extreme experiences (very good, very bad) and are unreliable on nuance. The traveler who found the service "attentive" may have experienced the same service I would describe as "formulaic."

The most reliable signal for a genuinely exceptional property is the opinion of someone who has been there recently, whose taste overlaps with yours, and who has enough comparison points to know what excellent actually looks like. This is what a well-connected advisor provides. It is also why the advisor relationship exists at a price point where the cost of a misaligned hotel selection is significant enough to justify the help.

The practical implication

When a client tells me they want a five-star property, I treat it as a starting point, not a specification. The rating tells me roughly where on the market they're targeting. It tells me nothing about whether a specific property will be right for the trip. The questions that follow — what kind of building do you want to sleep in, how important is food, do you want a property that connects to where you are or one that exists as a destination in itself, what does service mean to you — are the questions that lead somewhere useful.

The five-star category has become, essentially, a floor. It rules out a certain kind of bad experience. It does not reliably produce a great one. Knowing the difference — and knowing which properties within the category actually represent what the rating was supposed to mean — is a meaningful part of what travel planning at the high end should be.

A note on how we select properties

We don't lead with star ratings when selecting properties for client itineraries. We lead with fit: is this property intrinsic to its setting, does it have the service character the client is looking for, has recent performance matched the property's reputation. The star rating is a useful filter. It is not a recommendation.
