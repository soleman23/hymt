The Country

Morocco Is a Driving Country,
Not a City Break.

Morocco's window is October through April, and the strongest ends of it are October–November and March–April. A first trip runs 10 to 14 days: Fez and Marrakech want three nights each, and the Sahara costs four days round-trip from Marrakech. Drive it one way instead and you buy two of those days back. All nine of Morocco's UNESCO sites are cultural, not natural.

The two medinas are why most people come and the part most trips get wrong. Fes el-Bali and the Marrakech medina are working cities rather than heritage quarters: no vehicle access, and a street plan that predates the idea of a street plan. UNESCO dates the Fez one to the ninth century and lists it among the largest car-free urban areas anywhere. All nine of Morocco's World Heritage sites are cultural inscriptions, which is the honest shape of the country: you come for what people built and kept using.

Everything between the two cities is the actual trip, and it is reached by road. The Tizi n'Tichka pass over the High Atlas, the earthen ksar at Aït Benhaddou, the gorges at Dades and Todra, the dunes at Merzouga — none of it is a day trip from anywhere. Road distance is the most underestimated thing about planning Morocco, and the fix is routing rather than speed: go one way, Marrakech to Fez or the reverse, and never drive the same pass twice.

The north is a separate decision, not a bolt-on. Tangier, Chefchaouen, the Roman city at Volubilis and the imperial walls at Meknès sit within a few hours of Fez, and they are the argument for twelve days rather than nine. One season rule overrides all of it: from July into September the interior is punishing and the Atlantic coast is not. WMO climate normals put Marrakech's July and August daily highs around 37°C, with Essaouira ten to fifteen degrees below that. High-summer Morocco is the coast and the mountains, not the medinas and certainly not the dunes.

Six Places Worth the Driving Days

The map is deceptive. Fez and Marrakech look like a matched pair of city breaks and behave like opposites, and the passes, oasis valleys and dunes between them are where most of the trip actually happens. These six repay the road time.

Fès-Meknès
Fez
The older, denser and less commercial of the two great medinas, and the harder one to walk. The Qarawiyyin, founded in 859 and still teaching; the Bou Inania madrasa; the Chouara tannery from a terrace above the vats rather than through a shop. Take Fez early, while curiosity outruns fatigue.

The North: Tangier, Chefchaouen, Volubilis
A second Morocco trip, or a first one for travellers who want cities and history rather than dunes. What We Add to Morocco Planning

Two decisions decide most of how a Morocco trip feels: who guides you in each medina, and how the driving days are routed. Both are made months before departure and neither is visible on a booking site.

⬡
The Guide Is the Medina
Guiding is a licensed profession in Morocco, and the men working the medina gates offering to help are not licensed. Their route through the souks is a commission route: you will see four shops and think you saw a city. A licensed guide is there for access and refusal — which tannery terrace is working rather than staged, which madrasa is open that morning, which co-operative actually weaves. Jemaa el-Fna is on UNESCO's intangible heritage list for the storytellers and musicians who work it, the people a good guide can introduce you to.
⬡
Riads, Room by Room
A riad is a house built inward around a courtyard, usually five to ten rooms, and the variation inside one house is larger than the variation between two hotels. The courtyard rooms hear every conversation on the ground floor; the top-floor rooms are quiet and four flights up with no lift. There is no lobby, no midnight room service, and the arrival ends on foot or behind a handcart. That is the trade for sleeping inside the medina rather than beside it — right for two or three nights, wrong for a week. We book the specific room, not the property.
⬡
Driving Days, Honestly Counted
Morocco itineraries fail on arithmetic more than on taste. Marrakech to Merzouga is roughly 560 km over a mountain pass and through two gorges — nine to ten hours if you push it, two comfortable days if you stop where stopping is the point. Run it as a loop and four days of a ten-day trip go on a car to sleep one night on a dune. Route it one way and the same driving becomes the trip. We build the ground plan around that arithmetic first and fit the cities to it, which is why our Morocco itineraries have fewer days that feel like transfers.
⬡
Season by Latitude, Not by Country
Morocco does not have one climate and a good itinerary does not pretend it does. In the same August week, Marrakech sits near 37°C, a Merzouga camp is well past it, Imlil at altitude walks comfortably all day, and Essaouira needs a jacket after dark. In January the reverse applies: the southern light is the best of the year and the Atlas passes take snow. Which Morocco we plan starts with when someone can travel, and the honest July answer is a different country from the March one.
When to Go

Morocco by Season

The Travel Window
October – April
The whole country works at once, which is true in no other window. The medinas are walkable at midday, the Sahara nights are cold rather than dangerous, and the southern light through December and January is the best of the year. Two caveats: the High Atlas takes snow and a pass can close for a day, and desert nights in midwinter drop close to freezing, so a camp is chosen for its heating rather than its photographs. October–November and March–April are the strongest ends. Easter and the Christmas fortnight are when the good riads go six months ahead.
Best Overall Window
Late Spring
May – June
The last workable weeks inland and the best weeks in the mountains. Marrakech is hot by midday but manageable with early starts, the Atlas walking peaks as the snow line retreats towards Toubkal, and the coast is warming without the August wind at full strength. The desert is at the edge of sensible by late May and past it by mid-June. If the trip must fall here, front-load the medinas and the south, then climb or move west.
High Summer
July – September
Two different countries. Marrakech runs daily highs around 37°C and the desert is well beyond it, which makes the classic medina-and-dunes itinerary a bad idea rather than a hard one. The Atlantic coast is unaffected: Essaouira and the shore north of Agadir sit ten to fifteen degrees cooler under the Canary Current and the afternoon trade wind, and the High Atlas walks well all day. Planned as a coast-and-mountains trip with the medinas taken before nine, July is very good. Planned the other way it is the worst version of the country.

Morocco — FAQ

The questions that come up most when planning a first Morocco trip — timing, which medina to start in, what the Sahara costs in days, and who should be doing the driving.

Fez first, then Marrakech. Fez has the older and denser medina — UNESCO dates its founding to the ninth century — and it asks more of you: more walking, more stairs, more disorientation. Take it on day two of the trip rather than day nine, while curiosity still outruns fatigue. Marrakech afterwards reads as manageable rather than overwhelming, and it sits closer to the two soft landings, Essaouira and the Atlas foothills. The practical version is an open-jaw booking: in through Casablanca or Fez, out of Marrakech. Reverse the order and Fez arrives as more of the same, only harder.

Four days from Marrakech and back, or two if you drive it one way and finish in Fez. Marrakech to Merzouga is roughly 560 km over the Tizi n'Tichka pass and down through the Dades and Todra gorges: nine to ten hours driven straight, and two comfortable days if you stop where stopping is the point. Route it one way — Marrakech, Atlas, ksour, Merzouga, then north through the Ziz valley to Fez — and the driving stops being a penalty and becomes the reason to go. Under nine days, skip the Sahara and take the High Atlas: Imlil is ninety minutes from Marrakech, not two days.

A riad in the medina for the medina nights, and a hotel outside the walls for the pool days. A riad is a house built inward around a courtyard, usually five to ten rooms, and the only way to sleep inside the medina rather than beside it. Expect stairs and no lift, rooms that vary enormously within one house — the courtyard ones hear everything, the top-floor ones are quiet and four flights up — and the last two hundred metres on foot, because no car reaches the door. If anyone has a bad knee or a stroller, split the trip: two or three riad nights, then a hotel with a pool.

In Fez and Marrakech, yes — a licensed one, for at least the first full day in each. Guiding is a licensed profession here; the men at the medina gates offering to help are not, and their route through the souks is a commission route — you will see four shops and believe you saw a city. A licensed guide is there for access and refusal: which tannery terrace is working rather than staged, which madrasa is open, which co-operative actually weaves. Take him for day one, then walk the medina alone. Getting lost in Fes el-Bali on day two is a good afternoon. Getting lost on day one is not.

Use a driver for the long routes and hire a car only for the coast — Marrakech to Essaouira, or north of Agadir. The motorway linking Casablanca, Rabat, Fez and Marrakech is modern and easy; the roads this trip is actually made of are not. The Tizi n'Tichka is a mountain pass above 2,200 metres carrying freight, the gorge roads narrow to the riverbed, and the last stretch into a desert camp is sand. A driver is the person who knows when to leave Merzouga to reach Fez before dark. For city-to-city legs, take the train: ONCF publishes the timetable, and the Al Boraq high-speed line covers Tangier to Casablanca in about two hours ten.
