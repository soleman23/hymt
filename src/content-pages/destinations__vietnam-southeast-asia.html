The Region

Vietnam Holds Three Climates
At the Same Time.

Vietnam runs 1,650 km north to south and carries three climate zones at once, so a single best month does not exist. February through April is the only stretch that works from Hanoi to the Mekong Delta. Central Vietnam keeps its own rain window, September through December, peaking in October and November. End to end needs 13–15 days.

The country's own embassy in Washington puts it at 1,650 km from the northern border to the tip of the Mekong Delta, and about 50 km across at the narrowest point. That shape is the whole planning problem. Hanoi has a cold, damp winter and a wet summer. Da Nang and Hoi An sit under a rain window that belongs to no other part of the country. Ho Chi Minh City runs a straightforward wet and dry calendar. Any answer to "when should I go to Vietnam" that names one month answers for a third of it.

Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City are not two versions of the same city, and travellers rarely love both equally. Hanoi is older, denser and slower — guild streets still trading by trade, a coffee culture built on egg yolk and condensed milk, and a January that needs a jacket. Ho Chi Minh City is louder, hotter, newer, and better fed at the top end. If the trip holds one city, most first-time visitors belong in Hanoi: the day trips out of it are stronger and the food is more particular to the place.

The add-on question has a clear answer and most itineraries get it backwards. Angkor earns its days: three nights in Siem Reap gives you a 400-square-kilometre archaeological park, and the flight from either end of Vietnam is short enough not to cost a travel day at both ends. Luang Prabang is the harder call. It is a detour rather than a stop: it wants four nights and its own reason, and bolted onto a fortnight in Vietnam it takes more than it returns.

Central Vietnam and Luang Prabang
For a second trip, or a first that wants two countries rather than one country's whole length. Two nights in Hanoi, four split between Hue and Hoi An, then a flight to Luang Prabang for four more. Three Calendars, One Itinerary
A Vietnam trip rarely goes wrong on a hotel. It goes wrong by running the country in the wrong direction in the wrong month. Start in Hanoi in early November and work south and you meet central Vietnam at the peak of its rain, which nothing on the ground can fix. The same route in March meets none of it. We fix the order and direction against the month you can travel, then choose properties inside that. Where the month is fixed and wrong for all three regions, we say which third to cut.
⬡
The Bay Is a Boat Decision
Nobody remembers which bay they were in. They remember the boat, how many other people were on it, and whether the anchorage was quiet at six. That makes this a boat decision, and the boat market is genuinely hard to read from photographs: cabin counts run from four to forty, one operator name can cover three tiers of vessel, and the route decides whether you wake in a channel full of engines or a cove with nothing in it. It is one of the few places where a call to someone who has been aboard changes more than any hotel choice.
⬡
Guides Who Answer Follow-Ups
Vietnam's twentieth century is on the itinerary whether you schedule it or not — the Citadel at Hue, the Cu Chi tunnels, the museums in Ho Chi Minh City. The gap between a guide working from a script and one who has read widely and will take a hard follow-up question is enormous, and it is not something a booking form lets you request. The same holds at Angkor, where a guide trained in the archaeology turns a temple into the hydraulic city it belonged to. These are named requests, made months ahead.
⬡
Which Add-On Earns the Days
Angkor earns them. Luang Prabang usually does not, and you should hear that before you book. Siem Reap sits a short flight from either end of Vietnam, and three nights there is a second destination rather than an errand. Luang Prabang is farther, slower to reach, and worth four nights when it gets them — bolted on for two at the end of a fortnight it costs a travel day at each end and gives back an afternoon. Wanting both honestly means three weeks. If the trip is two, pick one and do it properly.
When to Go

Vietnam by Season

The Whole-Country Window
February – April
The one stretch where north, centre and south are all workable at once. Hanoi is warming and mostly dry, the central coast sits between its rain windows, and the south is still in its dry season. That is why every north-to-south itinerary here carries these months. It is also the busiest window: the good bay boats and the Hoi An tailors commit earliest, so a March trip is a decision made the previous autumn. Tet, the Lunar New Year, falls in late January or February depending on the year and wants planning around rather than through.
Best Overall Window
Central Coast Summer
May – August
The best months for the beaches at Da Nang and Hoi An, and the worst for walking anywhere at midday. The centre is dry and bright; the north is hot and humid with afternoon storms; the south is in its wet season, which usually means a heavy hour rather than a lost day. This is the window for a trip built on the central coast rather than the country's full length. Rates sit below spring and the bays are quieter, at the price of the heat and a storm risk rising from August.
Central Rain, Northern Winter
September – January
Months to plan around rather than write off. Central Vietnam's rain runs September through December and peaks in October and November, when the Thu Bon River backs into Hoi An's Ancient Town in most years. Vietnam's national forecaster issues the storm warnings worth watching, rather than a ten-day phone forecast. Meanwhile the north is cool and dry and at its best, and the south dries out from December. A trip held to Hanoi and the bay works well here. A north-to-south run does not.

Every other month favours one region and works against another, which is why a single-month answer to this question is usually wrong. October through April is right if the trip stays north. May through August suits a trip built on the central coast. Where the dates are fixed and bad for all three, cut a region rather than run the whole line badly.

Thirteen to fifteen days, with three internal flights. Eleven is possible and produces a trip of packing and unpacking; nine is a highlight reel. The workable shape is three nights in Hanoi, two aboard a boat in the bay, two around Hue, three in Hoi An, two in Ho Chi Minh City and one in the Mekong Delta, with flights placed so no good morning goes to an airport. Shortening rarely means dropping a place — it means dropping the second night everywhere, and Vietnam punishes that harder than most countries, because so much of what is good here happens before seven.

The exception is a traveller who wants the show caves and the large-vessel comfort only the main channel offers.
