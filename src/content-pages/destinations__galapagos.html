The Choice That Determines
What You Actually See.

The Galápagos archipelago contains thirteen major islands distributed across 28,000 square kilometers of Pacific Ocean — a geography that turns out to matter enormously for what kind of trip is possible. The eastern islands (Santa Cruz, Santiago, Bartolomé, North Seymour, Española) are accessible from a land base in Puerto Ayora and Baltra. The western islands (Fernandina, Isabela, the northern parts of Santiago) — which contain the most pristine wildlife environments and the species concentrations that justify the Galápagos's UNESCO listing — are only accessible by liveaboard. The choice between land-based and liveaboard determines which 40% of the archipelago you can actually visit.

The naturalist guide is the single most consequential planning decision in the Galápagos. The Galápagos National Park licenses approximately 400 naturalist guides, divided into three tiers based on training and language certification. A Class III naturalist — typically a Darwin Foundation-trained scientist who has spent decades in the field — produces a different experience from a Class I guide who has passed the basic license exam. The Class III naturalist on a small liveaboard reads the islands at a different level: explaining what Darwin was actually recording about adaptive radiation, identifying the specific finch species and the beak morphology that correlates with the food source on each island, distinguishing between male and female frigatebirds at fifty feet by silhouette.

The seasons are not a strong factor in choosing when to visit — the Galápagos has no genuinely bad season — but they do shift the emphasis of the trip. June through November (the cool, dry season, driven by the Humboldt current pushing up from the south) produces colder water, more aggressive sea lion activity, the best diving conditions, and the waved albatross nesting on Española. December through May (the warm, wetter season) brings calmer seas, sea turtle nesting on the beaches, and the peak of the land bird breeding season. We match the timing to the trip's emphasis.

Santa Cruz & Charles Darwin Research Station
The most populated island in the Galápagos and the main land base for travelers who choose the lodge format over the liveaboard. Southeastern Islands
Española Island
The waved albatross colony — the only nesting site in the world for the species, with approximately 30,000 birds returning to nest from April through December. Watching a pair of albatross perform the courtship dance (head-bobbing, beak-clattering, the wing display) is one of the great wildlife encounters of the islands. The blow hole at the cliff edge of Punta Suárez. The red-billed tropicbirds, the swallow-tailed gulls (the only night-feeding gull in the world), and the marine iguanas of Española that have evolved into red and green coloration during breeding season unlike the iguanas of any other island. The most wildlife-dense island in the archipelago.

Destination Photography · Fernandina
Western Islands
Fernandina & Isabela
The western islands accessible only by liveaboard: Fernandina is the most pristine island in the archipelago — no introduced species, no human settlement, and the largest marine iguana colony in the world (thousands of individuals piled together at Punta Espinoza, warming themselves before diving into the cold western current to graze the algae). The flightless cormorants of Fernandina are found nowhere else on earth. Isabela is the largest island, formed from six overlapping shield volcanoes — the Galápagos penguins (the only penguin species that lives north of the equator) and the cormorant colonies at Punta Vicente Roca. The lava landscapes of Sullivan Bay on Santiago show the islands at their geological youngest.

Destination Photography · Bartolomé
Central Galápagos
Bartolomé Island
The iconic view across Pinnacle Rock with the Galápagos penguins swimming in the bay below — the most photographed single view in the Galápagos, which still earns the attention even after the hundredth time you've seen the photograph. The 372 wooden steps to the summit viewpoint pass through volcanic moonscape that demonstrates the geological youth of the eastern islands. The snorkel below Pinnacle Rock is one of the most reliable encounters in the archipelago — penguins, sea lions, white-tipped reef sharks, and a high probability of one of the few green sea turtles that nests in the lower bay during the December–February season.

North Central
North Seymour & Baltra
North Seymour for the magnificent frigatebird colony in breeding season (the males inflate their red throat pouches to grapefruit size and call from the treetops, visible from the walking path at extremely close range), and for the land iguana dens that are walked past on the same trail. Baltra is the main entry point for travelers — the airport island, originally a WWII US Air Force base, now the primary commercial gateway to the Galápagos. A short channel separates Baltra from Santa Cruz, crossed by ferry in 10 minutes. North Seymour is typically the first or last island visited on a Galápagos itinerary.

Naturalist Guide Quality
The Galápagos National Park licenses approximately 400 naturalist guides in three tiers. A Class III naturalist (typically a Darwin Foundation-trained scientist with a graduate degree in ecology or marine biology) reads the islands at the species, behavior, and evolutionary biology level. A Class I naturalist points at iguanas. The difference is significant. The best liveaboards staff exclusively Class III naturalists; we know which boats and which lodges retain them, and we book based on this rather than on the marketing photographs.

Seasonal Timing
The Galápagos has no genuinely bad season — there is no time when wildlife is broadly absent. The cool dry season (June–November, driven by the Humboldt current) produces cold water (18–22°C), the best diving, more aggressive sea lion behavior, and the waved albatross nesting on Española. The warm wet season (December–May) brings calm seas, warm water (24–28°C), sea turtle nesting, and the peak of the land bird breeding season. The honeymoon couple, the photographer, the diver, and the family all get different optimal months. We match the timing to the trip.

Island Selection
Galápagos itineraries are tightly regulated by the National Park — each licensed ship operates a fixed route on a 14-day or 7-day rotation, and visits to specific landing sites are scheduled across operators. This means the choice of ship determines the choice of islands. Some routes prioritize the western islands (Fernandina, Isabela), others prioritize the northern outliers (Genovesa, Pinta), others stay closer to the central circuit. We map the itineraries against the islands you most want to see and book the ship whose route delivers your priorities.
When to Go

The Galápagos by Season

Cool Dry Season
June – November
The Humboldt current pushes north from the Antarctic, dropping sea temperatures to 18–22°C, increasing nutrient density, and concentrating wildlife activity. The best months for diving and snorkeling visibility. Sea lion activity is at its most playful and curious — pups in the water approach divers and snorkelers without hesitation. Waved albatross are nesting and dancing on Española (the species departs for the Pacific from December onwards). Cooler air temperatures (in the low 70s) and overcast skies are common. The most consistently rewarding window for wildlife-focused travel, and the period most serious naturalists and photographers prefer.
Best for Wildlife & Diving
Warm Wet Season
December – May
The warm Panama current brings calm seas, water temperatures of 24–28°C, and frequent short afternoon rain showers. Sea turtle nesting peaks January through March on the sandy beaches. Land bird breeding season is at its peak, with most species in active courtship and nesting display. Best months for family travelers and for travelers who prioritize beach time, calm seas (significantly less seasickness risk for liveaboards), and warm water snorkeling without wetsuits. The land iguanas are at their most active in the morning sun. Rainfall is rarely heavy and rarely sustained.
Year-Round Note
No Bad Season
Unlike most wildlife destinations, the Galápagos has no genuinely bad month. Wildlife is consistently present year-round and the resident endemic species (giant tortoises, marine iguanas, sea lions, Darwin's finches) are active at all times. Sea temperature, swell, and specific seasonal events (albatross nesting, turtle nesting, penguin activity) shift across the year, but no month closes the archipelago to meaningful travel. We match the season to the trip emphasis rather than steering all clients to one window.

Yes, for the most extraordinary diving. The Galápagos has world-class scuba diving — Kicker Rock (sea lions, white-tipped sharks, occasionally hammerheads), Gordon Rocks (the volcanic crater wall, hammerhead schools, mantas), and Wolf and Darwin Islands in the far north (the most concentrated marine megafauna diving on earth, accessible only on dedicated diving liveaboards).
