Bhutan Publishes Its Price
Before Anyone Asks.

Bhutan's two strong windows are late September to November and March to May. A first trip runs eight to ten days across Paro, Thimphu and Punakha; pushing east to Bumthang adds three or four. Every adult visitor pays a Sustainable Development Fee of US$100 per night on top of hotels, and a licensed guide is required throughout.

Bhutan is the one country on this site that publishes its own entry price. The Department of Tourism charges a Sustainable Development Fee of US$100 per adult per night, on top of hotels, guiding and transport, with concessionary rates for children and a separate one-off visa fee. That is not a booking fee dressed up. The money goes into national programmes including free healthcare and schooling, and it is the mechanism that holds arrival numbers where the government wants them. Verified August 2026; the rate and its concessions move with policy, so confirm before booking.

The second rule people bristle at turns out to be the best part of the trip. Bhutan's Department of Tourism states that visitors must be accompanied by a guide at all times, and in practice you travel with a guide and a driver for the whole itinerary. Read that as a restriction and you will resent it by day three. Read it correctly and it is a licensed interpreter, a monastery fixer and a road-conditions service assigned to you rather than to a coach of forty. Which individual you get matters more here than which hotel you booked.

Geography then decides the length. Paro, Thimphu and Punakha sit within a day's driving of one another and hold most of what a first visit wants; Phobjikha adds a night, and Bumthang is a long day further east over two passes. Nothing here moves fast — the roads follow rivers and ridgelines through a country that keeps a constitutionally mandated minimum of 60% forest cover. That is the trade. Bhutan is wrong for a traveller counting sights per day and close to perfect for one who is not.

Six Valleys, in Planning Order

Bhutan is planned by valley rather than by city, and the road between two valleys is usually a pass. The first four below make a complete first trip. The last two are the argument for a longer one.

Paro Dzongkhag
Paro Valley
The arrival valley, and the one most itineraries underrate. Taktsang is here, and so is Rinpung Dzong above the river and the National Museum in the old watchtower. Bhutan's tourism authority puts the valley floor at about 7,000 feet, which makes the first night a settling night whether you planned one or not.

Thimphu
A working capital rather than a set piece. Tashichho Dzong houses the government and the central monastic body inside the same walls, the weekend market runs on Bhutanese produce instead of souvenirs, and the archery ground is where the national sport is actually played rather than demonstrated.

Punakha
The warm valley, and the reason the switchbacks over Dochula are worth taking slowly. Punakha Dzong stands where the Pho Chhu and Mo Chhu meet, about two and a half hours' drive from Thimphu and several hundred metres lower, which makes it the comfortable night in a spring or autumn itinerary.

Phobjikha & Gangtey
A glacial bowl where black-necked cranes winter from mid-November to the end of February. Outside those months it is still the best valley walking in the west: open grassland, a seventeenth-century goemba on the ridge, and a night at real altitude that most itineraries leave out.

Bumthang
Four valleys and the oldest temples in the country — Jakar Dzong, Kurjey Lhakhang, and buckwheat where the west eats rice. It is a long day east of Punakha over two passes, which is exactly why most itineraries skip it and why the ones that do not come home describing a different country.

Haa Valley
The quiet one, reached from Paro over the Chele La pass and still the least visited corner of the west. It suits a second trip, or a first one with days to spare: two nights in a farmhouse, a ridge walk down through blue pine, and a valley that empties entirely by late afternoon.

Every Bhutan itinerary is built around the guide and driver first and the lodges second, because the guide is who decides whether a dzong reads as a fortress or as a working institution.

The Western Valleys, Unrushed
The first Bhutan trip, and the one I recommend for almost everybody. Two nights in Paro on arrival doing very little — Rinpung Dzong, the museum, an early night — then two in Thimphu for the dzong, the weekend market if the days line up, and an afternoon at the archery ground. Over Dochula to Punakha for two nights at the confluence, where the valley is warm enough to sleep properly. Back west with a night in Phobjikha if the season suits. Taktsang goes on the last full morning, by which point four nights of valley air have done their work and the climb is a walk rather than a test.

Bumthang Road · Bhutan
March – May · 12–14 Days
East to Bumthang by Road
The western circuit as the first half, then the drive most trips run out of days before attempting. Out of Punakha over the Pele La with a night in Phobjikha, then Trongsa — the ancestral seat of the royal family, on a spur above a gorge — before dropping into Bumthang for three nights. Four valleys, the oldest temples in Bhutan, and a food culture that changes noticeably once buckwheat replaces rice. Return by road with a different overnight, or fly Bumthang to Paro when the domestic schedule cooperates, which buys back a full day. Twelve days is the honest minimum; fourteen is the version that works.

Tsechu · Bhutan
Festival Dates Vary · 9–11 Days
A Tsechu, and the Valleys Around It
Built backwards from one fixed date. The tsechu calendar follows the Bhutanese lunar year, so Paro's spring festival and Thimphu's autumn one land on different Gregorian weeks each year and the current list sits with the Department of Tourism. Confirm the dates before anything else is booked, then build two days at the festival ground into the middle of a standard western circuit. Go early on the day, sit where your guide puts you, and stay for the mask dances rather than the opening. The smaller district tsechus are the better experience if crowds are a concern, and your guide will know which ones fall in your window.

Phobjikha · Bhutan
Nov – Feb · 8–10 Days
Cranes, Clear Air and Fewer People
Winter Bhutan is cold, bright and the quietest version of the country, and it is badly undersold. Black-necked cranes arrive in Phobjikha in mid-November and stay to the end of February, which is the one wildlife event on the Bhutanese calendar worth planning around. Three nights in the valley makes the difference between seeing the roost and watching it. Pair it with Punakha, which stays warm through the winter, and keep Thimphu and Paro short. Bring proper layers and expect unheated buildings in the monasteries. The mountain views in January are the best of the year, and you will share them with almost nobody.

What We Add to Bhutan Planning

The government has already fixed the fee and the guiding requirement. What is left to plan is the person, the pace and how far east the road goes — and those are the three things that decide the trip.

⬡
The Guide Is the Itinerary
Since a guide is mandatory anyway, the only question worth arguing about is which one. The range is wide and the price is identical at both ends. A guide who recites founding dates and a guide who can explain what a mask dance depicts, get you into a lhakhang while a ritual is running, and say honestly which monastery is not worth the hour are the same line item. So we ask for named individuals rather than a company, we say what the group actually wants — architecture, birds, walking, textiles — and we push back when the answer is whoever is free.
⬡
Altitude in the Right Order
Bhutan is not a high-altitude trip in the Andean sense, but the sequence still matters. Landing at Paro around 7,000 feet and climbing straight to Taktsang next morning is the standard mistake, and it turns the country's best walk into the hardest thing anyone does all week. Two valley nights first, Punakha in the middle to sleep low and warm, Taktsang late — that ordering costs nothing. Phobjikha and the passes are where people notice the air, so those go after the body has had a few days rather than before.
⬡
How Far East to Push
For most first trips the honest answer is: not past Punakha and Phobjikha. Bumthang is genuinely the cultural heart of the country and also a long driving day over two passes in each direction, so adding it to a nine-day trip converts roughly a third of the itinerary into road. Give it three nights or leave it for a second visit. The people who should push east have fourteen days, a tolerance for mountain roads, and more interest in temples than in comfort. We say which group you are in before you book.
⬡
Festival Weeks and Room Inventory
Bhutan has very few beds at the top end, and a tsechu week removes most of them at once. The lodge groups run eight to sixteen rooms per valley, so a festival date does not tighten the market gradually — it closes it. The order of operations is fixed: confirm the date against the official calendar, hold rooms in every valley on the route, apply for the visa and the fee, then price flights. Reverse any two of those steps in a festival window and you get the right dates in the wrong valleys.
When to Go

Bhutan by Season

Autumn
September – November
The clearest air of the year, settled weather after the monsoon, and the season that makes the mountain views reliable rather than lucky. It carries the biggest festival of the year in Thimphu, which is the reason capital rooms go first and the reason to fix your dates before anything else. Late October and November also open the crane season in Phobjikha. The trade is that this is peak Bhutan: the small lodge groups sell out valley by valley, and a late booking usually means splitting the party across properties or dropping a valley from the route.
Best Overall Window
Spring
March – May
The walking season, and the better half of the year for anyone who cares more about being outdoors than about long-lens mountain views. Rhododendron runs up the passes, the jacaranda comes out at Punakha Dzong, and the trekking routes open as the snow line retreats. Haze builds through May and the high peaks disappear behind it more often than in autumn. Paro's tsechu falls in this window, so the same booking pressure applies. If the trip includes Bumthang or any real walking, spring is the stronger of the two main seasons.
Green Season & Deep Winter
June – Aug · Dec – Feb
Two very different quiet seasons, both worth considering and neither for a first-timer in a hurry. The summer monsoon brings a few hours of rain most days, green valleys, working paddy fields and the lowest visitor numbers of the year; the high passes stay open and the trekking is good above the cloud. Midwinter is cold, dry and exceptionally clear, with the cranes in Phobjikha to the end of February and unheated monastery interiors that catch people out. Bring layers and take the quiet as the point.

What Bhutan Actually Costs

A government fee per night, plus a day rate
The Sustainable Development Fee is published and fixed, but it is a government levy sitting on top of a day rate that swings by a multiple between a valley guesthouse and the five-lodge circuit.

  • The government fee — US$100 per adult per night, US$50 for ages six to eleven, none under six

It is a real hike rather than a photo stop, and it takes most people about two hours up. Bhutan's tourism authority describes the climb to Taktsang as a steady ascent of some 3,000 feet above the Paro valley floor, reaching roughly 10,000 feet, with a cafeteria near the halfway point and a long flight of stone steps at the end. Ponies go as far as the cafeteria and no further; everyone walks down on their own legs. Put it on the last full morning rather than the first. Anyone unhappy with uneven steps and exposure should say so at the planning stage, because there is a good half-day alternative and no good improvisation at the trailhead.
