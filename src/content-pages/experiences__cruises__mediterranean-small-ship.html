Why Small Ships

Small Ships, Smaller Harbours

Ship size decides a Mediterranean cruise more than the route does. A small ship, here meaning roughly 30 to 300 guests, can call at island towns such as Korčula and Hvar, while big ships compete for capped daily slots at Dubrovnik and Santorini. The best windows are May–June and September–October. To hire the whole boat instead, see private yacht charters.

This page is about a cabin on a shared ship running a scheduled sailing: you book the dates the line publishes, and the other guests are strangers. A crewed yacht charter is the other format, one group, one crew and a route agreed each morning, and it has its own page.

The Mediterranean is where the ship-size question bites hardest. The famous ports now ration cruise passengers by the day, and the quieter harbours along the Dalmatian coast, the smaller Cyclades and southern Italy are reachable mainly by a ship short enough to fit them.

Small ships carry fewer facilities and move more in a swell, and the fare per night is usually higher. The return is time: shorter tender queues, ports that are not on a megaship schedule, and evenings ashore after the day visitors have gone. The Decision

What Ship Size Changes

Port rules and ship figures from the sources linked, checked October 2026.

⬡
What Counts as Small
There is no official cut-off, so look at the guest count. The Variety Cruises fleet carries 34 to 72 guests; SeaDream I carries 112 in 56 staterooms. This page draws the line at about 300.
⬡
Dubrovnik's Daily Limit
The Dubrovnik Port Authority books cruise calls on a "2+1" principle, two large ships in port and one anchored off the Old City, which in most cases limits cruise passengers to 8,000 a day.
⬡
Santorini's Tender and Cap
Every cruise ship anchors in the caldera, small ones included. The Municipal Port Fund of Thira's berthing policy caps cruise visitors at 8,000 on any one day in 2025 and 2026.
⬡
Time Ashore
Read the published arrival and departure times before the port list. A ship that leaves at six and one that stays until midnight are two different evenings in the same harbour, and a smaller ship lands and recovers its guests faster.
⬡
What You Give Up
One or two restaurants instead of a dozen, no theatre or water park, fewer cabin categories, and more motion in open water. For travellers who come for the coast rather than the ship, that is the point.

The Details

What a Small-Ship Booking Includes

On a ship this size the cabin and the days either side matter more than the onboard extras.

⬡
Ship and Cabin by Deck Plan
With a few dozen cabins on board, position matters: midships and low down for anyone who feels the motion, away from the anchor and the tender platform for anyone who sleeps lightly.
⬡
Land Nights Either Side
A night in Dubrovnik, Split, Athens or Sorrento before boarding, so a late flight never costs the sailing, and the hotel on the far end chosen for the town rather than the airport.
⬡
Shore Days Around the Ship's Clock
Private guides or a driver timed to the published all-aboard, for ports such as Korčula, Hvar or Santorini where the ship's own excursion is not the best use of the day.
⬡
Flights and Embarkation Day
Flights into the embarkation port, not the nearest hub, transfers to the marina or quay, and a check of what the line includes before you pay for it twice.
When to Go

The Mediterranean Small-Ship Calendar

Small-ship seasons in the Med run roughly April to October. The shoulder months either side of the summer peak are the ones to aim for.

Late Spring
May – June
Long days, island towns open, and the busiest weeks of the cruise year still ahead.
Best Window
High Summer
July – August
The fullest ports and the daily caps at their most tested. In the Aegean, the Etesians, the summer north winds the Hellenic National Meteorological Service describes, can roughen crossings and reorder a route.
Early Autumn
September – October
The sea at its warmest and the crowds thinning. By late October some Dalmatian island towns are closing, Hvar first, as the Mediterranean in October explains.

What a Small-Ship Sailing Actually Costs

Priced per cabin per sailing, not per night
A small-ship fare is set per cabin for one departure, and it moves with the cabin category, the week of the season and how many share the cabin, so a nightly two-adult band would describe none of the sailings on this page.
What actually sets the number
  • Cabin supply: SeaDream I has 56 staterooms in all, so the best categories on a popular departure sell first
  • The week: shoulder departures in May, June, September and October against the July and August peak
  • What the line includes, from drinks and excursions to gratuities and port charges
  • Flights into the embarkation port and hotel nights either side of the sailing

There is no official definition; this page means a ship carrying roughly 30 to 300 guests. At the small end, the Variety Cruises fleet carries 34 to 72 guests, and SeaDream I carries 112. Some lines call ships this size yachts, but you are still buying a cabin on a scheduled sailing, not the whole boat.

On a small ship you book a cabin on a fixed route; on a charter you hire the whole boat and crew. The cabin is the better buy for a couple or a small family. The charter suits a group that can fill every cabin and wants to set the route each morning, and it is priced per boat per week, as the yacht charters page explains.

May, June, September and early October are the best months for most routes. July and August bring the fullest ports and, in the Aegean, the summer north wind. On the Dalmatian coast, late October is a gamble on the islands: some towns, Hvar especially, start to close. The case for the shoulder months is made in the case for shoulder season.

Yes: every cruise ship at Santorini anchors in the caldera and lands its guests by tender. The Municipal Port Fund of Thira serves them at the Fira old port and Athinios, and from Fira the way up is the cable car, the 587 steps or a donkey. A small ship's advantage is a shorter tender queue, not a berth.

It can: the meltemi, or Etesians, is the seasonal north wind of the Aegean summer. The Hellenic National Meteorological Service describes it blowing mainly across the Aegean. On a windy day a small ship may swap the order of two islands or shelter in a lee bay. Anyone prone to seasickness should take a low midships cabin and sail in May, June or September.

By small ship if you want several islands in a week without repacking; by land if you want three nights in one place. A ship strings together Mljet, Korčula, Vis and Hvar with no ferry timetables. Land and ferry gives you longer evenings in fewer towns. Many trips do both: a sailing, then two or three nights in Dubrovnik or Split.
