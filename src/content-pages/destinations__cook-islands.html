The Islands

Aitutaki Is Why You Go.
Rarotonga Is Where You Land.

May through October is the dry window, and a first Cook Islands trip runs 9–10 nights. Give Aitutaki at least five of them and Rarotonga the rest: the lagoon is the reason to come, and a day trip does not cover it. Exactly one resort in the country has overwater bungalows. Cyclone season runs November to April.

The Cook Islands are sold as one destination and planned badly as one. Fifteen islands, about 15,000 residents, and two of them carry nearly every visitor: Rarotonga, where the international flights land and roughly seven in ten Cook Islanders live, and Aitutaki, one domestic hop north. They are not variations on each other. Rarotonga is a working island with a town, a bus timetable and a narrow fringing lagoon. Aitutaki is a triangular atoll wrapped around a lagoon that photographs the way it actually looks.

Almost every complaint about the Cook Islands is really a complaint about Rarotonga booked as if it were Aitutaki. The lagoon on Rarotonga is thin, the bottom goes from clean sand to seagrass within a few hundred metres, and the swimmable stretches sit on the southern coast around Muri and Titikaveka rather than in front of the town. Booked for what it is, a green volcanic island with a 32 km coast road, an interior worth walking and a Saturday market, it holds three or four nights easily. Booked as a beach week, it disappoints on the first afternoon.

The country is also not a quieter Bora Bora, which is the other framing that causes trouble. French Polynesia has overwater villas by the hundred across several islands; the Cook Islands have one resort with them, on a private motu off Aitutaki. What you get instead is a place with a resident population going about its week, no resort monoculture, and rates that sit below the French Polynesian equivalent. What you do not get is nightlife, shopping, or much open on a Sunday. That suits a specific traveller and quietly annoys everyone else.

Where the Nights Actually Go

Fifteen islands, and for almost every trip, two of them. Splitting those two properly is the whole planning exercise. These six places are where the nights go, and the ratio between them decides more than the hotel does.

Aitutaki
Aitutaki Lagoon
The reason the country is on anyone's list. A shallow triangular lagoon ringed by motu, wadeable a long way out in places, and the whole exclusive economic zone around it sits inside Marae Moana, a marine park legislated in 2017. Take the lagoon cruise twice, not once.

One Foot Island
Tapuaetai, the sandbar in the far corner of the lagoon that every cruise stops at. The one-room post office stamps passports with a foot, which is frankly a gimmick and also the only stamp of its kind anywhere. Book a boat that gets there ahead of the fleet or after it leaves.

Rarotonga
Muri & Titikaveka
Rarotonga's southern coast, and the only part of the island that reads as a beach week. Muri's lagoon is broad and shallow with four motu offshore; Titikaveka is quieter with the better snorkelling. Depth and bottom change stretch by stretch, which is why the specific property matters more here than the island.

The Cross-Island Track
The interior, which most visitors never see. Roughly seven kilometres from the north coast to the south, past Te Rua Manga, the 413-metre volcanic plug known as the Needle, finishing at Wigmore's Falls. Three to five hours depending on the mud, and the best half-day on Rarotonga by some distance.

Rarotonga
Avarua & the Market
The capital, such as it is: one low street on the north coast, a small harbour, and the Punanga Nui market on Saturday mornings for 'ei katu, ika mata and produce. Sunday belongs to the church here. A Cook Islands Christian Church service is open to visitors and the singing is the reason to go.

Southern Group
Atiu
The third island, for travellers who want something other than a lagoon. Atiu is raised makatea limestone honeycombed with caves; Anatakitaki holds the kopeka, a swiftlet that navigates the dark by echolocation. Add a small coffee industry and the tumunu bush-beer tradition. Air service is limited and books out.

The Split That Works
Two nights on Rarotonga's south coast at the start, which is what the arrival timing tends to force anyway and which gets the jet lag out of the way somewhere with a lagoon in front of it. Then five nights on Aitutaki: two lagoon days rather than one, a morning on the far motu before the day boats arrive, and enough unscheduled time that the weather can move a plan without wrecking it. Back to Rarotonga for the last two nights, timed so the Saturday market and the cross-island walk both land. That sequence protects the connection home, which a single-night Rarotonga finish does not, and it puts the two lagoon days early enough that a blown-out morning can still be rescheduled rather than written off.

Cook Islands and French Polynesia
The two Polynesias in one trip, using the Rarotonga–Papeete service rather than backtracking through Auckland. What We Add to Cook Islands Planning

Four decisions carry this trip, and three of them are made before anyone books a hotel. Get the ratio and the routing right and the properties are the easy part.

⬡
The Ratio Between Two Islands
The single most common Cook Islands itinerary is seven nights on Rarotonga with one day trip to Aitutaki, and it is close to backwards. The lagoon that justifies the distance gets six hours; the island that does not gets six days. Aitutaki wants five nights minimum, which is what it takes for two proper lagoon days plus a weather day. Rarotonga wants three or four, and earns them on the coast road, the interior and the Saturday market rather than on its beaches. We start every plan with that ratio and work the flights around it.
⬡
Routing Decides the Trip
Rarotonga has one international airport and a thin set of routes into it, mostly through Auckland, with Sydney, Brisbane, Papeete and a seasonal Christchurch service alongside. That thinness has consequences. Miss a connection and the next opportunity may be days out rather than hours. The domestic hop to Aitutaki carries a lower checked-baggage allowance than the long-haul sector, which surprises people at the counter. And arrivals into Rarotonga cluster at hours that decide whether the first night is a night or a shower. The routing is settled first here, not last.
⬡
Which Stretch of Which Lagoon
On Rarotonga, "beachfront" and "swimmable at low tide" are unrelated claims, and the gap between them is a few hundred metres of coast. The southern arc holds the good water. The north and parts of the east give you a lagoon you can wade in and not much you would swim in, and photographs taken at high tide will not tell you which you are booking. On Aitutaki the question is whether a property sits on the lagoon side, which way it faces for the afternoon trade wind, and whether the boat leaves from its own jetty or a drive away. Those are property-by-property answers.
⬡
Saying Who It Does Not Suit
The Cook Islands are wrong for a meaningful number of people who ask about them, and the honest conversation costs five minutes and saves a holiday. There is no shopping, no nightlife past an island night, no restaurant scene to work through, and Sunday closes most of what there is. Families with teenagers who need stimulation tend to struggle by day four. Travellers who want the full overwater-villa resort machine should be looking at French Polynesia or the Maldives, and we will say so. What is left is the group this country is genuinely excellent for.
When to Go

The Cook Islands by Season

Dry Season
May – October
Lower humidity, steadier southeast trades, and the clearest water on the Aitutaki lagoon. This is when the country is worth the distance and it is priced accordingly, with July and August the tightest weeks for the small Aitutaki properties and the domestic seats to reach them. Humpback whales pass through roughly July to October and are seen from shore on Rarotonga's west coast in a good year. The trade wind that keeps the temperature honest is the same one that makes the kitesurfing season, and the same one that can put a chop on the lagoon for a morning.
Best Overall Window
The Edges
April & November
Two months that often behave like the dry season with better availability, and occasionally do not. Both sit inside the official cyclone window, both bring more humidity than June, and both regularly deliver a fortnight anyone would take. The trade is real rather than theoretical: you gain room in the properties that are full in July, and you accept a wider spread of possible weather. For a second trip to the region, or for anyone who has to travel outside school holidays, these are the months worth arguing for.
Wet Season
December – March
Warmer, wetter, and inside the South Pacific tropical cyclone season, which runs November to April in the regional seasonal outlooks. Most of those weeks pass without an event, rain tends to arrive in heavy short bursts rather than all day, and rates soften. What changes is the downside: a named system can shut the domestic air link for days, and insurance stops being optional. Book these months deliberately, with cover in place and a plan that can lose two days, or book May instead.

Give Aitutaki at least five nights and Rarotonga three or four, not the reverse. Five on Aitutaki is what it takes to get two full lagoon days plus one day the weather is allowed to take, which is the difference between seeing the lagoon and spending time on it. Rarotonga earns its three or four nights on the 32 km coast road, the cross-island track, the Saturday market and an island night, none of which need a beach. Booking it the other way round is the most common Cook Islands mistake, and it produces a holiday that was fine rather than one that was the point.

The day-trip product exists, sells well and delivers a few hours on the water in the middle of the day, arriving with every other day boat and leaving before the light turns. What it cannot give you is the lagoon early or late, when the colour is doing what the photographs promised and the sandbars are empty. Aitutaki's lagoon stands comparison with any in the Pacific, and the country receives a small share of French Polynesia's traffic, so the water is genuinely emptier. What French Polynesia has that the Cooks do not is scale: dozens of overwater properties, several islands with real hotel choice, a serious food scene in Papeete, and the ability to build a two-week trip without repeating yourself. If the villa itself is the holiday, go to French Polynesia. If the lagoon and the quiet are the holiday, the Cooks win, and it is not close.

Anyone who wants nightlife, shopping, a restaurant scene, or a resort that fills every hour for them. This is a country of roughly fifteen thousand people. Rarotonga has one small town, Aitutaki has a handful of places to eat, and Sunday closes most of both for church. Teenagers who need stimulation tend to run out of it by day four, and travellers who measure a trip by how much they did will find the answer here is not much. The people it suits are the ones for whom that is the entire appeal, and they are the ones who come back.

Rarotonga is the only international gateway, and Auckland is the hub most itineraries route through. Sydney, Brisbane, Papeete and a seasonal Christchurch service make up most of the rest, and the schedule is thin enough that a missed connection can cost days rather than hours. Inside the country, Air Rarotonga operates every domestic sector, including Aitutaki and Atiu, with a lower checked-baggage allowance than the long-haul flight. Schedules and routes change season to season, so confirm current services with the airline and with Cook Islands Tourism before committing to dates.

Most visitors do not need a visa and are granted a 31-day permit on arrival. The conditions attached to it are the part people miss: a passport valid well beyond the departure date, proof of onward travel, and accommodation booked before you land. That last one catches independent travellers who intended to sort a room out on the island. Requirements differ by nationality and change without much warning, so treat this as orientation rather than authority. The current rules are published by the Cook Islands Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Immigration, checked August 2026; verify against your own government's current travel advice before booking.
